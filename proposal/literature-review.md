## 2.1  Current Research / 研究现状

The core question of this study is how Pakistani teachers of Chinese recontextualize Chinese-language teaching materials in classroom practice, and how their agency is achieved or constrained within the ecological conditions of their work. Existing research can speak to parts of that question. It has not yet been asked to answer it as a whole.

Three terms have to stay apart, or the literature will look complete when it is not. Recontextualization is the pedagogic mechanism visible in materials-in-use (Bernstein, 2000). Ecological teacher agency is the achievement that makes that mechanism possible, restricted, or redirected (Biesta & Tedder, 2007; Priestley, Biesta, & Robinson, 2015). Localization is the name the Pakistan conversation already uses. In this study it is the process and outcome when recontextualization orients materials toward a Pakistani classroom. It is not a third theoretical framework. It is not a pre-set goal. It is not a test of whether the lesson was better.

### 2.1.1  Recontextualization of teaching materials

Bernstein (2000) treats knowledge as transformed when it moves from the sites where it is produced into the sites where it is taught. It is selectively appropriated, relocated, and refocused. An official recontextualizing field of syllabuses and examinations sets terms. A pedagogic recontextualizing field of publishers and education faculties mediates those terms. Evaluative rules feed back into what may be selected. An HSK-oriented series already arrives as such an object. The classroom is a further site, not the first one.

Yu (2015) uses the word more loosely, for university teachers’ local handling of curriculum reform in China. That usage is close to practice. It also lets recontextualization mean any local handling of a text from elsewhere. Follow it and this study becomes a study of implementation. The materials disappear.

Empirical work then splits by method. Guerrettaz and Johnston (2013) observed an intact language class and showed that a coursebook can become the de facto curriculum, organising turns and topics. Guerrettaz et al. (2022) and Harwood (2021) recast that finding as an agenda: materials as content, consumption, and production. Z. Li (2020) and Li and Xu (2020) show, in Chinese higher-education English classrooms, that use is a process, not a reading of the page. These studies establish the window. They do not study Pakistani teachers of Chinese, and they do not take an HSK-oriented series as the classroom object.

A second strand studies adaptation as professional work. Rathert and Cabaroğlu (2022) treat adaptation as a principled response to mismatch between a generalised book and a particular class. Rathert and Cabaroğlu (2021) show, with two English teachers, that use can remain coursebook-led when conditions restrict change. Hanifa and Yusra (2023), with the follow-on paper Hanifa et al. (2024), document addition, omission, substitution, and local cultural substitution under time, resource, and examination pressure. The two papers are one programme. Mohamad Uri and Abdul Aziz (2024) show, with imported CEFR textbooks in Malaysia, that distance from the book raises workload. Gray (2000) and Song (2024) add that teachers also censor, and that following the book can be a professional stance.

Criado (2023) is the limit this proposal has to keep. Teachers altered a large share of activities while remaining largely faithful to sequence. Some procedural change made lessons more teacher-fronted. Neupane (2025) reports that stated willingness to adapt often exceeds what is done. Toledo-Sandoval (2020) shows that even locally produced Chilean ELT books still needed classroom work. The local on the page and the local of the class were not the same construction. A move-count can look like localization and still leave the prescribed course as the curriculum.

Alvunger (2018) joins Bernstein’s device to teachers’ curriculum work in Sweden. It is a near-miss. The setting is not Chinese-language education. The population is not Pakistani.

### 2.1.2  Ecological teacher agency

If recontextualization is the operation, the next question is whether it can be done. Bandura (2001) treats agency as individual capacity. Beliefs matter. The inference that teachers who follow the book “lack agency” does not. A committed teacher in an HSK-led course can still have almost no room to change the sequence.

The ecological account relocates the problem. Emirbayer and Mische (1998) treat agency as temporally embedded: iterational, projective, and practical-evaluative dimensions sounding together. Biesta and Tedder (2007) treat it as an achievement in contexts-for-action. Priestley, Biesta, and Robinson (2015) specify that account for teachers’ work with prescribed curriculum. Autonomy is a condition. It is not a synonym for agency (Priestley et al., 2016). Beliefs are cultural resources. They do not suffice without professional discourse in which alternatives can be spoken (Biesta, Priestley, & Robinson, 2015). The 2015 book, the 2015 beliefs article, and Priestley et al. (2012, 2016) are one programme. The Scottish cases are analogues. Tao and Gao (2021) map conceptualisations of language teacher agency. They are a map, not a second working model. This study does not take “promote teacher agency” as its aim.

Empirical studies of prescribed textbooks converge on restriction. L. Wang (2022) distinguishes constrained, transformative, and progressive agency among English teachers under reform in China, and locates the difference in examination pressure and school culture. Yan and Weng (2026) name a pattern that is immediately relevant to an HSK-led course: teachers can often only add. Tao and Weng (2025) describe ideological support with shackled practice. Dao et al. (2025), with two Vietnamese English teachers at classroom level, code acceptance, adaptation, and resistance. Chen and Shu (2026) distinguish approval, compliant agency, and persistent agency. Bao et al. (2020) is the CFL-adjacent bridge, among teachers of Chinese in Belarus. It still does not study Pakistani teachers of Chinese, and it does not take HSK materials as the object of classroom recontextualization.

Restricted work still counts as agency. Interview-reported belief is not enacted mediation. The close studies remain chiefly EFL reform in China and Vietnam.

### 2.1.3  Localization in international Chinese-language education

Localization has no Bernstein genealogy. The word is kept because Pakistani policy and scholarship already use it.

Product studies recover official recontextualization already inscribed in global Chinese textbooks. Yasasri (2024) shows, through corpus and critical discourse analysis of books used in Sri Lanka, exclusion of South Asian cultures and local languages. Dassanayake (2023) converts a related critique into a checklist. Both can show what was available to be taught. Neither can show what was taught.

A later strand treats localization as teacher work. Zhao (2020), Zhao (2024a), and Zhao et al. (2024b) are one Australian programme in which students’ everyday practices become Chinese-learning content. Ling et al. (2023) describe bilingual teachers in Australian schools building from students’ worlds rather than only from the book’s China. Loo (2026) shows that localization of borrowed global curricula can be left to teachers, weakly supported and reactive.

Translanguaging sits next to this strand. It is not automatically localizing. Q. Zhang et al. (2020) and D. Wang (2022) show coordinated language use supporting explanation and participation in CFL settings. Nan et al. (2023) name a recontextualization of translanguaging in Indonesian Mandarin teaching. Language choice may be a means of moving a book into a class. It is not the whole operation. It is not Pakistan data.

A teacher can contextualize an item and still transmit the book’s sequence and examination logic (Ling et al., 2023). A teacher can also recontextualize by stripping the local (Gray, 2000). Localization is one possible bearing of the operation, not its definition. Pakistan is not in the teacher-work set.

### 2.1.4  Chinese-language education in Pakistani universities

Pakistan is not an unstudied country in this field. It is under-studied in the respect this title names.

C. Wang (2022) maps Pakistani Chinese teaching as a programme system. Claims from that dissertation remain provisional until the PDF is read. Hanif (2023) documents Confucius Institute expansion, volunteer teachers, shortage of local experts, and weak local materials. Khan et al. (2022) show a gap between mandatory Chinese policy and classroom reality, especially class size and medium of instruction. Azeem et al. (2022) report weak curriculum uniformity, English-annotated textbooks, and the burden on teachers to add or delete content. Ali and David (2022) place Chinese in Sindh’s language ecology. Naheed (2026) surveys 55 local teachers and reports heavy use of *HSK Standard Course*.

Lu et al. (2024) and Lu and Hanif (2025) examine *HSK Standard Course* in Pakistan. They are one programme, not two independent studies. Methods are literature review, questionnaires, and teacher interviews. The remedy they propose is product-side: Urdu annotations, cultural comparison, localized modules. HSK here is an institutional condition of teachers’ work. It is not the research object of this proposal. These studies describe perceived fit. They do not observe everyday recontextualization. They do not compare the prescribed page with what was actually taught.

## 2.2  Critical Commentary / 研究评述

The conversations above do not fail for lack of publication. They fail, for this title, at the join of object, population, and method.

| Conversation | Typical method | Typical setting | What it can show | What it cannot show here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Materials-in-use | Classroom observation | ELT / language classes | The book as de facto curriculum | Pakistani teachers of Chinese with HSK materials |
| Adaptation | Case, interview, some observation | ELT in Turkey, Indonesia, Malaysia | Moves, workload, coursebook-led use | Whether a busy move-count transformed knowledge |
| Product CFL | Textbook analysis, checklist, CDA | Global Chinese books, often Sri Lanka | Official selection already in the book | What was taught |
| Ecological agency | Qualitative case, often interview-heavy | China and Vietnam EFL; Belarus CFL bridge | Restricted, addition-only, compliant forms | This population and this materials object, joined |
| Pakistan CFL | Programme studies, survey, interview | Pakistani universities; *HSK Standard Course* | Perceived misfit; HSK as a widely used series | Observation plus materials comparison |

Textbook analysis and classroom observation are not interchangeable evidence. Yasasri (2024) and Dassanayake (2023) recover the official layer. Guerrettaz and Johnston (2013) recover interaction. Lu et al. (2024) recover talk about fit. None of those, alone, is recontextualization as enacted practice.

Interview-reported willingness is a poor proxy for practice. Criado (2023) and Neupane (2025) already show the discrepancy inside ELT. A Pakistan study of localization beliefs would reproduce that discrepancy as if it were the finding.

Analogues cannot be stacked until they look like this population. Dao et al. (2025) and Yan and Weng (2026) show that observation of textbook-mediated agency is possible. The method exists. The language object and the setting do not. Alvunger (2018) and Nan et al. (2023) occupy parts of the conceptual join in Sweden and Indonesia. They do not cancel the Pakistan problem.

## 2.3  Research Gap / 研究空白

Previous studies have separately examined ecological teacher agency in the use of prescribed language textbooks, chiefly among EFL teachers in China, Vietnam and related curriculum-reform settings, and the adaptation or recontextualization of imported and global teaching materials, including ELT cases in Malaysia, Indonesia and rural Southeast Asia and CFL textbook or contextualization studies in Sri Lanka, Australia and related sites. However, limited empirical research has investigated recontextualization of Chinese-language teaching materials and ecological teacher agency in a single design, and Pakistani teachers of Chinese are almost absent from both literatures. In particular, insufficient attention has been given to classroom observation plus materials comparison that would show how localization is produced in everyday teaching rather than received as textbook production, and to the institutional, pedagogical and professional conditions that shape what Pakistani teachers of Chinese adapt, how they adapt it, and why.

This proposal therefore does not evaluate whether HSK materials fit Pakistan. It does not offer a new textbook as its product. It asks four questions that follow from the gap:

1. What recontextualization practices do Pakistani teachers of Chinese enact when using Chinese-language teaching materials (addition, omission, substitution, modification, reordering, supplementation, translanguaging, cultural filtering, or faithful transmission)?
2. How do teachers justify these decisions in relation to learners, materials, pedagogy, culture, language resources, assessment, time, and professional identity?
3. In what ways is teacher agency achieved, restricted, or redirected across the iterational, projective, and practical-evaluative dimensions of ecological agency?
4. Which nested conditions (classroom, collegial and institutional, examination and HSK policy, professional community, and wider sociocultural setting) enable or constrain teacher-mediated localization?
