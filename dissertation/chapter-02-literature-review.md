# Chapter 2  Literature Review

The materials that enter a Chinese-language classroom in Pakistan are rarely written for that classroom. They are compiled, sequenced, and examined for a general international learner; they travel under the authority of a syllabus and, very often, of the HSK. Policy documents and programme studies in Pakistan already have a name for what this travel fails to do. They call it localization, and they usually locate the remedy in a future book: an Urdu gloss, a cultural module, a national series. What disappears from that story is the work already happening when a teacher opens the prescribed page, decides what to say in Urdu or English, skips a dialogue, inserts a local example, or follows the book tightly because the examination will not wait. This chapter is concerned with that work. It asks how the research conversations that could explain it—materials as they are used, teachers as they achieve or fail to achieve agency, Chinese as it is taught outside China—have been assembled, and where they have not yet been asked to speak to Pakistani teachers of Chinese.

Those conversations exist, but they have been conducted largely apart. One body of work has shown that prescribed language textbooks are not delivered intact: teachers omit, add, substitute, and reorder, and the book often becomes the de facto curriculum. Another has shown that whether such moves are possible is not only a matter of belief or skill, but of conditions of possibility and constraint, described most usefully as ecological teacher agency. A third has shown that teachers of Chinese mediate materials, culture, and multilingual repertoires, sometimes by drawing local life into the lesson. A fourth has documented the growth of Chinese-language education in Pakistan, the wide use of HSK-oriented series, and a recurrent call for localization. Previous studies have separately examined ecological teacher agency in the use of prescribed language textbooks, chiefly among EFL teachers in China, Vietnam and related curriculum-reform settings, and the adaptation or recontextualization of imported and global teaching materials, including ELT cases in Malaysia, Indonesia and rural Southeast Asia and CFL textbook or contextualization studies in Sri Lanka, Australia and related sites. Limited empirical research has investigated recontextualization of Chinese-language teaching materials and ecological teacher agency in a single design, and Pakistani teachers of Chinese are almost absent from both literatures. Insufficient attention has been given to classroom observation plus materials comparison that would show how localization is produced in everyday teaching rather than received as textbook production, and to the institutional, pedagogical and professional conditions that shape what Pakistani teachers of Chinese adapt, how they adapt it, and why.

The claim of this chapter is therefore smaller than the claim of the thesis. It is a claim about a research conversation:

> Previous research has examined ecological teacher agency in prescribed-textbook settings and the recontextualization of imported language materials as largely separate conversations; neither conversation has produced an empirically grounded account of how Pakistani teachers of Chinese recontextualize Chinese-language teaching materials in class.

If that claim holds, a qualitative study from an ecological perspective is not a relabelling of “materials fit.” It is an attempt to see localization where it is actually made or blocked. The chapter proceeds by establishing recontextualization as the pedagogic mechanism visible in materials-in-use, ecological agency as the achievement that makes that mechanism possible, restricted, or redirected, and localization as the name the Pakistan conversation already uses—the process and outcome when recontextualization orients materials toward a Pakistani classroom, not a pre-set goal and not a test of whether the lesson was “better.” Localization is not treated here as a third theoretical framework. The working model is reserved for Chapter 3. Chinese-language scholarship on 教材再语境化, 教师能动性, 教材使用, and 国际中文教育 belongs to the same argument; it is not an optional supplement to the English-language discussion that follows.

## 2.1  Materials Recontextualization: Concepts and Empirical Evidence

The phenomenon this thesis studies is not the textbook as a finished object, and not the gap between that object and a Pakistani “context” conceived as something already there. It is what happens to Chinese-language teaching materials when they are moved into a classroom: what is selected, relocated, silenced, added, reordered, or otherwise transformed. Materials arrive already officially recontextualized. Teachers’ further recontextualization is the operation. Mismatch between a standardized series and local learners is a starting condition of that operation, not its finding. A count of adaptation moves is a trace of it, not a theory of it. The subsections that follow first fix the concept, then bound it against neighbouring terms, then name the levels at which the literature already sees it, and only then turn to empirical studies of materials-in-use and of adaptation as professional practice.

### 2.1.1  Origins and Definitions

Recontextualization is not a synonym coined for localization. It names, in Bernstein’s sociology of education, what happens to knowledge when it is moved from the sites where it is produced into the sites where it is taught. Knowledge is not transferred intact. It is selectively appropriated, relocated, and refocused as it is converted into pedagogic communication (Bernstein, 2000). The pedagogic device is, in that sense, a theory of transformation rather than of delivery. Between the production of knowledge and its classroom reproduction lies a recontextualizing field, itself divided. An official recontextualizing field—state agencies, inspectors, prescribed syllabuses, examination authorities—sets the terms on which knowledge may become curriculum. A pedagogic recontextualizing field of education faculties, publishers, journals, and advisers mediates those terms, sometimes in tension with the official field, sometimes in alignment with it. Pedagogic discourse, on this account, is a principle for appropriating other discourses and bringing them into a special relation with one another for the purpose of their selective transmission and acquisition. Evaluative rules complete the device: they regulate what will count as a legitimate realisation of the knowledge so recontextualized. What is taught is therefore never simply “the subject.” It is the subject as it has been delocated from one context and relocated in another, with some meanings privileged, others weakened, and still others made not to appear—and as it will later be recognised or not recognised by an examination.

That origin matters for Chinese-language teaching in Pakistan because the materials in use already arrive as recontextualized objects. An HSK-oriented series is not Chinese language and culture in some unmediated sense. It is language, culture, and pedagogic knowledge that have already been selected for a general international learner and for an examination: which vocabulary is sequenced, which cultural scenes are staged, which skills are tested, which languages of instruction are assumed, and which local lives are not represented. The HSK does not sit outside this process as a later test of teaching. It is already part of the official recontextualization, because evaluative rules feed back into what may be selected and what must be preserved. The classroom is a further recontextualizing site, not the first one. To evaluate the book as if it were the beginning of the story is to miss the official work already inscribed in it. To treat the teacher as a mere implementer of that book is to miss the further work that still has to be done, or that is refused, when the page meets a particular class.

Language-education research has taken the term up more loosely, and the looseness is itself instructive. Yu (2015), studying university teachers’ enactment of curriculum reform in China, treats interpretation, re-interpretation, and recontextualization as what teachers do with central policy: reform is not implemented so much as made locally in the interplay of beliefs, students, and institutional conditions. That usage is empirically close to practice. It also shows how easily the term can drift toward a general word for “what teachers do with something from elsewhere,” without holding on to Bernstein’s insistence that knowledge itself is transformed, silenced, or re-ordered in the move. The present review keeps the stricter sense. Recontextualization is the pedagogic operation by which Chinese-language teaching materials, and the knowledge, language, and culture they carry, are selected, relocated, silenced, added to, reordered, or otherwise transformed when they are moved into a Pakistani classroom. Adaptation moves—addition, omission, substitution, modification, reordering, supplementation—are traces of that operation. They are not the operation itself. A teacher may change a great deal and still leave the original pedagogic message intact; another may follow the book closely and, in doing so, already be making a recontextualizing choice about what this class, this examination, and this university will treat as Chinese.

### 2.1.2  Recontextualization and Its Neighbouring Concepts

Because the word travels, it is easily confused with the terms that sit next to it in materials research. The distinctions are not terminological fussiness. They decide what can be seen in a Pakistani Chinese class, and what will be missed if the wrong word is asked to do the work of the title.

Adaptation is the nearest neighbour and the most tempting substitute. It names a set of observable moves: teachers omit, add, substitute, modify, reorder, simplify, and write supplements (Rathert & Cabaroğlu, 2021, 2022). Those moves are indispensable as traces. They tell an observer that something happened to the book. They do not, by themselves, tell what knowledge was transformed, which meanings were silenced, or whether the lesson still carried the book’s original pedagogic message under a busier surface. A count of adaptations can look like localization and still be textbook-led. Recontextualization asks a further question: not only what was done to the activity, but what happened to the knowledge, language, and culture the activity was meant to carry.

Enactment and implementation belong to a neighbouring policy vocabulary. They are useful when the object is a reform document, a new curriculum, or a set of standards that teachers “put into practice.” Yu’s (2015) study sits in that vocabulary even as it uses the word recontextualization. The difficulty for this thesis is that Chinese-language materials are not only policy objects. They are specific texts—pages, dialogues, exercises, audio, tests—handled minute by minute. To treat them only as the local enactment of a national or institutional policy is to lose the grain at which recontextualization is visible: this item kept, that cultural note skipped, this Urdu explanation inserted, that HSK drill preserved because the test will ask it.

Materials-in-use is the empirical window, not a synonym for the operation. Guerrettaz and Johnston (2013) showed that a coursebook can become the de facto curriculum and can organise classroom interaction itself: who speaks, about what, in what order, with what rights to depart from the page. That is a claim about the classroom ecology in which materials live. Recontextualization is what teachers (and, in interaction, students) do to knowledge and text inside that ecology. Without the window, the operation is inferred from interviews or from the book alone. Without the operation, the window shows that the book matters but not how meanings were moved.

Contextualization, as Ling et al. (2023) use it in Australian CFL classrooms, describes teachers who build lessons from students’ sociocultural worlds rather than only from the book’s China. That practice may be localizing. It is not identical with Bernstein’s transformation of knowledge. A teacher can contextualize a vocabulary item with a local example and still transmit the book’s sequence, its examination logic, and its cultural hierarchy almost unchanged. Conversely, a teacher can recontextualize without “bringing in the local” at all: by teaching to the test, by stripping cultural content, by replacing Pakistani life with a generic “international” Chinese (Gray, 2000). Relocating a text can delocalize it. That is why localization cannot be treated as the automatic outcome of recontextualization, and why the two words are not interchangeable. Localization is the name the Pakistan conversation already uses, and, in this study, the process and outcome when the operation orients materials toward a Pakistani classroom. The full debate about that name belongs in 2.3. What must be held here is only the boundary: recontextualization is the mechanism; localization is one possible bearing of that mechanism, not its definition.

Two further uses of the word sit at the edge of this boundary and should not be allowed to collapse it. Textbook- or corpus-level studies of global Chinese materials, as in Yasasri (2024) and Dassanayake (2023), can show what was available to be taught—which cultures appear, which languages are assumed, which local lives are absent. They recover official recontextualization already inscribed in the book. They cannot show what was taught. Nan et al. (2023) use “recontextualization of translanguaging” for the relocation of a multilingual pedagogy into Indonesian Mandarin teaching. Language choice may be a means of recontextualizing a book; it is not the whole operation. The point of these neighbouring uses is not to police vocabulary for its own sake. It is to keep the title’s object in view: what Pakistani teachers of Chinese do to materials in class, not what a corpus does to “the local,” and not what a pedagogy does when it changes its name.

### 2.1.3  Forms and Levels of Recontextualization

The literature already sees recontextualization at more than one layer, even when it does not always name the layers as such. Official recontextualization is inscribed before the teacher enters the room: in the syllabus, in the HSK as an institutional condition, in the prescribed series, in the examination that will later evaluate what the book was taken to mean. Teacher recontextualization is the further transformation that happens in planning and in materials work—what is prepared, photocopied, translated, skipped, or written alongside the page. Interactional recontextualization is what actually happens with students: the moment-by-moment taking up, silencing, or recasting of the text in talk, gesture, and other languages. Without all three, “classroom practice” collapses into document analysis or into self-report. A content analysis of *HSK Standard Course* can recover the official layer. An interview can recover what teachers say they do at the teacher layer. Only observation together with comparison of the materials actually used can show whether those layers coincide, diverge, or contradict one another in interaction.

Alvunger (2018) is the study that comes closest to joining Bernstein’s device to teachers’ work. In Swedish curriculum making, teachers appear as agents who borrow and combine content across spaces rather than as couriers of an official text. That move matters because it extends recontextualization downward from the official field toward the classroom. It is also a near-miss for this thesis. The setting is not Chinese-language education; the population is not Pakistani teachers of Chinese; the object is not an HSK-oriented series. The layers are named here as the literature uses them. They are not yet a coding model. That work belongs in Chapter 3.

Several debates follow from the layering, and they are named rather than settled. The first is whether recontextualization is always localizing. Official recontextualization for “the world” can be the opposite of localization; teacher recontextualization can follow the examination rather than the class; interactional recontextualization can strip the local as readily as it can invite it. The second is whether faithful transmission is a failure of recontextualization or itself a recontextualizing choice. Song (2024) treats following the book as a professional stance—evaluation, adaptation, exploitation—not only as a deficit of imagination. A teacher who transmits the HSK sequence intact may be deciding, under time and assessment pressure, that the book *is* the curriculum. That decision still relocates the text: it makes this class into an examination class. The third is who has the right to recontextualize. Official fields may tightly regulate what may be changed. Teachers’ spaces for manoeuvre may be narrower than their beliefs (Priestley et al., 2012). The right to add a local example is not the same as the right to reorder a course, drop a unit, or teach in Urdu. These debates cannot be resolved by definition. They can only be resolved, for this title, by seeing what teachers actually do, at which layer, under which conditions. That is why textbook analysis, teacher report, and classroom interaction are not interchangeable evidence of the same thing.

### 2.1.4  Empirical Research and Its Limitations

Empirical research on language-teaching materials has moved the object of inquiry from the book as a finished product to the book as something teachers and students do things with. The educational consequences of a textbook, on this view, depend on use, not on product quality alone. Guerrettaz and Johnston (2013) showed, in a language classroom ecology, how a coursebook can organise interaction itself: it becomes the de facto curriculum, allocating turns, topics, and legitimate departures from the page. Guerrettaz et al. (2022) and Harwood (2021, 2026) recast that insight as a research agenda. Materials must be studied as content, as consumption, and as production. If what a book does educationally depends on what is done with it, then evaluating the book in isolation cannot explain localization, however careful the content analysis. Content analysis is not thereby useless. It can show what was available to be recontextualized—the official layer already discussed. It cannot show what was recontextualized. Studies of teachers’ enactment of materials in Chinese higher-education English classrooms make the same point from the other direction: use is a process, not a reading of the page (Z. Li, 2020; Li & Xu, 2020; Li & Li, 2021). Related work on teachers as users, analysts, and designers, and on materials use as professional learning, belongs here as grouped support rather than as a second narrative (D. Li, 2023; Song, 2024; Li et al., 2021; Bouckaert, 2019; Li & Gao, 2025; Matsumoto, 2019; Kim, 2023).

Mismatch is the starting condition that this literature keeps rediscovering when standardized or imported materials travel. Rathert and Cabaroğlu (2022) treat textbook adaptation as a principled response to a felt or identified mismatch between the book and the learners in front of the teacher. The mismatch is not an accident of poor selection only; it is a structural consequence of writing for a generalised learner and teaching a particular class. Toledo-Sandoval (2020) shows how stubborn that consequence is. Even when a book already claims to represent local culture, teachers still add local references, because “the local” on the page and the local of the class are not the same construction. Dassanayake (2023) makes the parallel case for global Chinese textbooks in Sri Lanka: first language, local culture, translation, cost, and regional need are systematically under-attended in the product. The point for this thesis is narrower than a catalogue of misfit. Mismatch is what Pakistani teachers of Chinese already live with when an HSK-oriented series enters the room. It is the beginning of the process the title names, not the finding the title promises.

Teachers respond to that condition by working on the book. They add, omit, substitute, modify, reorder, simplify, and write supplements. Rathert and Cabaroğlu (2021, 2022) distinguish coursebook-led use from teacher-led adaptation and supply a usable set of moves for seeing the difference. Hanifa and Yusra (2023) and Hanifa et al. (2024) show those moves in Acehnese and Indonesian classrooms, including local cultural substitution, under time, resource, and examination constraints. Mohamad Uri and Abdul Aziz (2024) show, at larger scale with imported CEFR textbooks in Malaysia, that cultural and linguistic distance is accompanied by increased workload: adaptation is professional work, not a free exercise of preference. Rathert and Cabaroğlu (2021) supply the necessary counterpoint from the other end of the same problem. Teachers can remain coursebook-led when institutional and material conditions restrict what they can change. The book is then not merely a resource. It is a constraint that organises what may be said and done. Song (2024) refuses to treat that constraint only as a failure of imagination. Following the book—evaluating it, adapting it, exploiting it—is itself a professional stance.

Read together, these two strands of evidence do not say the same thing, and the difference is the reason this section cannot stop at a list of techniques. The materials-in-use studies show that the book is already doing pedagogic work before the teacher “adapts” it: it structures interaction, time, and what counts as the lesson. The adaptation studies show that teachers nevertheless work on the book, but that this work is uneven, costly, and often still coursebook-led. The synthesis is therefore not “teachers adapt, therefore they localize.” It is that materials both enable and constrain practice, and that the visible traces of change have to be interpreted against the book’s continuing hold on sequence, examination, and legitimate knowledge.

The most important qualification in this family is that frequent change is not the same as deep transformation. Criado (2023) found teachers altering a large share of activities while remaining largely faithful to textbook sequence; procedural change even made some lessons more teacher-fronted. Grouped studies of technique combinations and textbook dependence corroborate the pattern (Pratiwi et al., 2020; Gray, 2000; Neupane, 2025). A Pakistani Chinese class in which the teacher translates, skips a cultural note, and adds an Urdu example could therefore look highly adaptive and still leave the HSK course as the curriculum. Gray’s (2000) older observation that teachers censor as well as adapt cultural content belongs to the same caution: not every move toward or away from the page is a move toward the local. Teachers’ reported willingness to adapt, moreover, often exceeds what they do (Neupane, 2025). Interview-only designs will reproduce that discrepancy as if it were the finding. The relevance for this thesis is direct. A study that counted “adaptations” in Pakistani Chinese teaching could produce an active record and still miss whether teachers were recontextualizing materials for these learners or merely rearranging a prescribed HSK course.

The limits of the family follow from what it can see and from whom it has seen. It can describe the window (materials-in-use) and the traces (adaptation moves). It cannot, by a move-count, show what knowledge was transformed, silenced, or made to mean something else. That further question is the question of recontextualization. The close studies, with the partial exception of Dassanayake’s product-level work on Chinese textbooks, are not studies of Pakistani teachers of Chinese, and they are not studies of HSK-oriented series as the object of classroom practice. They are analogues: ELT and, at the edge, CFL textbook evaluation in other places. Analogues can motivate a design and can lend a vocabulary of moves. They cannot stand in for the population and the material object named in the title. It might be said that materials research has already explained localization as adaptation. Granted: the field has left behind a purely product logic, and it has shown that teachers work on books. Even so, adaptation traces are not the pedagogic operation, observation of English-textbook use is not observation of Chinese-language teaching in Pakistani universities, and a mismatch between global materials and local learners is a starting condition, not the phenomenon this thesis has to explain.

## 2.2  Ecological Teacher Agency: Concepts and Empirical Evidence

Whether recontextualization is possible is an ecological achievement, not a personal trait and not a driver used “in order to localize.” Restricted and addition-only forms still count. “Why teachers do it” is practical-evaluative reasoning (RQ2), not a synonym for agency.

### 2.2.1  From Individual Capacity to Situated Achievement

Agency entered educational research as an answer to a sociological problem: if structure is powerful, how do people ever do anything new? The word remains overworked, and three families of definition should not be collapsed.

Social-cognitive theory treats agency as the capacity to exercise control through intentionality, forethought, self-regulation, and self-reflectiveness, including personal, proxy, and collective modes (Bandura, 2001). That tradition takes teachers’ beliefs and efficacy seriously. It also slides easily into a claim that teachers “lack agency,” as if the difficulty were a missing personal attribute. A teacher with strong efficacy can still have almost no room to change an HSK-led course.

A second family treats agency as a synonym for autonomy, the right or freedom to decide. Priestley et al. (2016) distinguish the two: autonomy is a condition that may or may not support agency; agency is what is achieved, or not achieved, in action. A teacher may be formally free to adapt materials and still not achieve agency, or may achieve agency inside a tightly prescribed book.

The third family is the ecological account this thesis takes up. Biesta and Tedder (2007) relocated agency into adult learning: it is not a property a person possesses but something achieved in the interplay of routine, purpose, judgement, resources, and contexts-for-action.

A fourth family, which Hardman (2025) insists must not be treated as a slogan interchangeable with the third, distributes agency more widely. Relational and new-materialist accounts, and sociomaterial studies of classroom materials, show books and objects structuring interaction rather than lying inert (Guerrettaz & Johnston, 2013; Matsumoto, 2019; Kim, 2023). The distinction is consequential. Ecological teacher agency is about teachers’ achievement in conditions. Strong sociomaterial accounts make the book a co-agent. This review keeps teachers as the agents of recontextualization and treats materials as conditions and resources, not as co-agents in that stronger sense.

### 2.2.2  The Temporal-Relational Conception of Agency

Emirbayer and Mische (1998) disaggregated agency into a temporally embedded process of social engagement—the chordal triad of iteration (the past, habit, prior paths), projectivity (the future, imagined alternatives), and practical evaluation (present judgement amid contingency). The triad is named here, not operationalized.

That temporal disaggregation matters for this title. A Pakistani teacher’s biography, hoped-for class, and in-the-moment judgement are not one “attitude toward localization.” Iterational resources, projective purposes, and practical-evaluative judgements can pull in different directions in the same lesson. Priestley and colleagues made the triad usable for curriculum making; that uptake is the subject of 2.2.3.

### 2.2.3  The Ecological Model: Dimensions and Conditions

Priestley and colleagues made the ecological account usable for curriculum making. Agency varies with beliefs and with environmental conditions of possibility and constraint; it requires professional discourse and supportive institutional cultures, not only individual conviction; and input regulation (prescribed content and materials) and output regulation (steering by results) shape how much space for manoeuvre teachers actually have (Priestley et al., 2012, 2016). Teachers are inevitable mediators of prescribed curriculum; creative mediation is distinguished from strategic compliance (Priestley et al., 2016). Beliefs are cultural resources for agency, but they do not suffice without professional discourse and supportive cultures (Biesta, Priestley, & Robinson, 2015). Leijen et al. (2020) connect the model to reflection as a way of strengthening purpose and the capacity to adapt curriculum.

Nested maps of micro, meso, exo, macro, and chrono systems discipline talk of “context” (Namgung et al., 2023; Chen, 2023). Tao (2021) is a caution for university settings: the university, not only the nation-state, may be the strongest filter of policy. The nested ecology of Pakistani Chinese teaching is assembled in Chapter 3. Here the literature is reviewed, not recoded.

Teacher agency, in this review, is therefore an ecological achievement: the temporally shaped interplay of iterational resources, projective purposes, and practical-evaluative judgements in nested conditions of possibility and constraint. It is not a trait, not a synonym for autonomy, and not measured by how strongly a teacher endorses localization. Recontextualization practices are among its visible traces.

### 2.2.4  Empirical Studies of Agency in Materials and Curriculum Use

Empirical work on prescribed-textbook reform has already given the ecological definition operational forms. These studies are reviewed here once, as findings. Their limits for this thesis are stated in 2.2.5.

L. Wang (2022) gives the account operational form under examination and school-culture pressure: constrained, transformative, and progressive agency. Yan and Weng (2026) name a restricted, “addition-only” form highly relevant to HSK-led courses. Dao et al. (2025) code acceptance, adaptation, and resistance among Vietnamese teachers implementing new textbooks. Chen and Shu (2026) distinguish approval, compliant agency, and persistent agency. Tao and Weng (2025) describe teachers who support reform ideologically but remain shackled in practice. Tran (2022) and Truong (2023) trace the chordal triad in Vietnamese new-textbook implementation.

Time, syllabus, supervision, and student realities appear repeatedly as practical-evaluative conditions (Rivera Cuayahuitl & Pérez Carranza, 2015; Huang & Guo, 2024). Identity reconstruction through new textbooks and through materials-development work shows that materials change who teachers can be, and that who they have been shapes what they dare to change (J. Zhang et al., 2025; Liyanage & Walker, 2023; Li & Gao, 2025). Nested maps appear as empirical tools as well as conceptual ones (Namgung et al., 2023; Chen, 2023; Tao, 2021). Bao et al. (2020) already bring identity, community, and materials use together among Chinese teachers in Belarus. That is a CFL-adjacent bridge to the agency framework.

These studies agree that prescribed materials are mediated unevenly. They differ on how they name the forms—restricted, compliant, persistent, resistant, constrained, transformative. Together they show that interview-reported belief is not the same as enacted mediation, and that restricted or addition-only work is still agency.

### 2.2.5  Limitations of Existing Teacher-Agency Research

The close studies in 2.2.4 are not re-summarized here. Their limit for this thesis is not that they failed to describe agency in textbook use. It is that they cannot stand in for Pakistani teachers of Chinese.

The population is wrong. The close studies are chiefly EFL teachers under national English-textbook reforms in China, Vietnam, and related settings. Bao et al. (2020) is a CFL-adjacent bridge, still not Pakistani teachers of Chinese, still not HSK-oriented series as the object. Analogues can guide later coding. They cannot replace this population.

The method is often interview-heavy. Where observation exists, it is observation of English (or other) textbook implementation, not of Chinese-language materials in Pakistani universities. Reported willingness to adapt often exceeds what is done. Interview-only designs of “localization beliefs” will overstate what teachers can do. Restricted agency is still agency; analysis must include faithful transmission and strategic compliance, not only transformative change.

The conceptual ceiling is that ecological textbook-agency research rarely joins Bernstein-type recontextualization as the named pedagogic operation. Alvunger (2018) does that join in Swedish curriculum making — a near-miss named in 2.5.2, not a finding to unpack again here.

Family B can explain uneven mediation of prescribed books. It cannot yet explain how Pakistani teachers of Chinese recontextualize Chinese-language materials, or how that work is achieved or blocked. It might be said that ecological agency in textbook use is already studied. Granted: the method exists. Even so, the population and the material object do not.

## 2.3  Localization in International Chinese-Language Education

Localization is the name the field already uses. In this study it is the process and outcome when recontextualization orients materials toward a Pakistani classroom — not a pre-set goal, not a “better lesson” test, and not only a new book. It is not a third theoretical framework.

### 2.3.1  Definitions and Boundaries

Localization entered language-materials research as a remedy for imported books. The older and still dominant story is a production story: global or metropolitan textbooks misfit local learners, and the solution is a national series, a local edition, cultural substitution, or L1 annotation. That story remains the default in Pakistan CFL writing, which concludes toward Urdu annotations, cultural comparison, and localized modules (Lu et al., 2024; Lu & Hanif, 2025; Naheed, 2026). Those studies are Pakistan evidence in 2.4; they are named here only as illustrations of the product discourse.

A later strand treats localization as something teachers do, not only something publishers produce. Tian and Weninger (2026) show that “the local” in English-textbook research is constructed in several, sometimes contradictory, ways, and that the research is geographically uneven. Loo (2026) is the sharpest warrant for looking at teachers rather than at new national books: in Southeast Asian settings, localization was largely left to teachers, with little institutional support, and was reactive, fragmented, and politically shaped.

Localization has no Bernstein genealogy and should not be given one. The word is retained because it is the word Pakistani policy and scholarship already use. The analytic term for what teachers do is recontextualization. The analytic term for whether they can do it is ecological agency.

### 2.3.2  Localization and Its Neighbouring Concepts

Localization is not a synonym for translation, adaptation, or contextualization. Translation and L1 glosses are empirically central in CFL classes; they are not automatically localizing. Adaptation is a set of moves; it can be busy and still textbook-led. Contextualization, as Ling et al. (2023) describe it, builds from students’ worlds; it may localise, but it is not the whole operation.

Translanguaging is a neighbouring practice, not automatically a localizing one. Chinese, English, local languages, pinyin, and multimodal resources are often coordinated in CFL classes. That mediation can support explanation and participation. It can also reinforce a dominant language when testing and materials pull that way (Liu, 2025), and transitional translation is not the same as a transformative pedagogy (Yuan & Lee, 2024). Close studies of CFL translanguaging belong in 2.3.4. The caution belongs here: language choice on a page and language choice in a lesson are not the same evidence, and neither is by itself localization.

### 2.3.3  Dimensions of Localization

The field talks about localization along several dimensions: linguistic (L1 annotation, medium of instruction, scripts), cultural (festivals, food, place names, “local culture” on the page), pedagogical (task type, sequence, teacher and learner roles), and institutional (exams, syllabus, who chooses the book, training). These are ways the literature talks. They are not this study’s research questions, and they are not a return of five dimensions of HSK fit.

The dimensions can be thin. Adding a Pakistan-themed dialogue can leave HSK sequence, pedagogy, and language hierarchy untouched. “The local” may mean nation, ethnicity, everyday life, or an imagined community (Tian & Weninger, 2026). Teachers add local references even when books already claim to represent local culture (Toledo-Sandoval, 2020); they also filter or censor cultural content (Gray, 2000). More localization is not always better. Teachers may change a great deal and remain textbook-led (Criado, 2023). Localization can be conservative, examination-serving, or hierarchy-preserving.

### 2.3.4  Teacher-Led Localization and Its Limitations

Chinese-language education cannot be treated as ELT with the language swapped in. Yasasri (2024) shows, through corpus and critical discourse analysis, that global Chinese textbooks can exclude South Asian cultures and local languages and can enact one-way cultural adaptation. Dassanayake (2023) converts that critique into a 37-item checklist covering language, culture, pedagogy, affordability, accessibility, and local inclusiveness. Both are valuable, and both remain closer to product evaluation than to classroom agency.

Zhao (2020, 2024a), Zhao et al. (2024b), and Ling et al. (2023) show a different move in Australian schools: students’ everyday practices, school spaces, talents, and funds of knowledge become Chinese-learning content, including through translanguaging. Zhao (2024a) and Zhao et al. (2024b) are related Australian work on localizing Chinese curriculum by the same first author; the 2024b article is co-authored with Wei Li and Danping Wang. They are treated here as one programme of classroom localization, not as two independent Pakistan-relevant datasets. Duangmanee and Waluyo (2024) show Thai schools creating local materials and activities while remaining dependent on external Chinese-language expertise. Geng and Lu (2026), Han (2026), and Wang and Bale (2024) extend the picture of Chinese teachers working outside China under resource constraint and uncertain assessment.

In Chinese-language classrooms, English, local languages, pinyin, images, gestures, and Chinese are often coordinated to explain meaning, sustain participation, and connect new content to what learners already know. Q. Zhang et al. (2020) show translanguaging supporting explanation, elicitation, classroom management, and participation in CFL despite monolingual institutional norms. D. Wang (2022) shows, over an eighteen-month ethnography with Chinese teachers in Hong Kong, that translanguaging can support ethnic-minority learners through interpretive, managerial, and interactive practices. Puspitasari et al. (2024) describe Indonesian higher-education CFL lecturers using Chinese, English, Indonesian, pinyin, visuals, and peer languages together. Nan et al. (2023) reframe translanguaging as goal-oriented mediation for comprehension, memory, engagement, and linguistic bridging—an explicit “recontextualization of translanguaging.” Grouped studies extend the same point across CFL/CAL and immersion settings (Han, 2022; Tian, 2021; Zheng, 2019; Huang et al., 2025; S. Zhang et al., 2025). The implication is double. Urdu, English, regional languages, and Chinese are likely ecological resources. Localization cannot be defined as printing Urdu glosses in a new edition.

South and Southeast Asian cases show that local adaptation is valuable and that it is often left to individual teachers. Hanifa and Yusra (2023) and Hanifa et al. (2024) remain the closest classroom comparisons: imported English materials, local cultural substitution, multilingual support, and constraint by time, training, and examinations. Mohamad Uri and Abdul Aziz (2024) add scale and workload. Loo (2026) shows, across borrowed global curricula, that localization can be reactive, fragmented, and politically shaped, with little institutional support. Grouped comparative studies corroborate the pattern (Neupane, 2025; Thapa & Upadhayaya, 2026; Toledo-Sandoval, 2020; Saemee & Nomnian, 2021).

CFL localization is therefore thinkable as teacher work and as textbook revision. The teacher-work side is documented in Australia, Thailand, Indonesia, Norway, and related sites. Pakistan is not in that set. Analogues can motivate a study; they cannot occupy the gap. It might be said that localization of Chinese materials is already studied. Those studies analyse textbooks, checklists, or contextualization outside Pakistan; they do not show ecological agency among Pakistani teachers of Chinese.

## 2.4  Chinese-Language Education in Pakistani Universities

Pakistan CFL research describes programmes and perceived fit. It does not describe classroom recontextualization plus ecological agency. This section is the context in which teachers work, not a China–Pakistan history.

### 2.4.1  Development and Institutional Settings

Chinese-language education in Pakistan has a documented programme history, institutional variety, and a materials landscape in which HSK-oriented series are widely used. C. Wang’s (2022) doctoral dissertation maps Pakistani Chinese teaching as an international-communication and programme system rather than as classroom recontextualization of a particular book; the dissertation PDF was not available for this review, and bibliographic identity is confirmed from citing literature. Jabbar (2025) reviews historical development and current landscape. Hanif (2023) documents Confucius Institute expansion, dependence on volunteer teachers, shortage of local experts, and the lack of standardized, locally adequate materials. Khan et al. (2022) show a gap between mandatory Chinese policy at a higher-education institution and classroom reality, especially class size, medium of instruction, and intended outcomes. HSK functions here as an institutional condition of teachers’ work, not as a document to be evaluated in isolation.

### 2.4.2  Pakistani Teachers of Chinese

The title centres teachers. The available literature describes them mainly as a workforce problem: shortage of local experts, reliance on volunteer teachers, uneven training, and uncertain professional status (Hanif, 2023; Azeem et al., 2022). Naheed (2026) surveys 55 local teachers and reports professional-development needs alongside heavy use of *HSK Standard Course*. These studies establish who is in the classroom in institutional terms. They do not show how those teachers recontextualize materials.

### 2.4.3  Learners’ Linguistic and Educational Backgrounds

Learner backgrounds are treated thinly, only as they explain teachers’ practical-evaluative conditions. Ali and David (2022) place Chinese in Sindh’s language ecology of English prestige, Urdu nationalism, and regional identity. Aftab and Abbasi (2024) supply learner-side evidence of perceived difficulty, especially in reading and writing. Iftikhar et al. (2024) report opportunity-driven motivation alongside regional differences; detailed claims from that study await closer reading of the PDF. None of this is a second research object. It is part of what teachers read when they decide what to do with the book.

### 2.4.4  Teaching Materials and Multilingual Mediation

Naheed (2026) reports that 78.18% of surveyed local teachers used *HSK Standard Course* as their main instructional resource. Azeem et al. (2022) report weak curriculum uniformity, English-annotated textbooks, and the burden placed on teachers to add or delete content. Lu et al. (2024) and Lu and Hanif (2025), related publications rather than independent evidence, recommend Urdu as support. Those recommendations are not a substitute for observed Pakistani classroom practice. The CFL translanguaging family in 2.3.4 cannot be imported as if it were Pakistan data.

### 2.4.5  Reported Localization Needs and Challenges

Two closely related publications from the same Guangdong University of Foreign Studies group examine *HSK Standard Course* in Pakistan and must not be counted as independent evidence. Lu et al. (2024) combine a literature review, student questionnaires, and interviews with Pakistani Chinese teachers, and they recommend Urdu annotations, cultural comparison, and localized modules. Lu and Hanif (2025) is a follow-on article—same first author, same corresponding author, same textbook object—based on document analysis and further teacher interviews. It cites Lu et al. (2024) and turns the emphasis toward pedagogical challenges and Urdu as a support language. Hanif (2023) belongs to the same authorial programme as background on Confucius Institutes. These papers describe perceived cultural, linguistic, and pedagogical gaps. They propose materials-production remedies. Fit, culture, and L1 glosses appear here as *reported* needs and as starting conditions, not as findings of this thesis.

### 2.4.6  Limitations of Pakistan-Specific Research

Pakistan is not an unstudied country in CFL. It is under-studied in the specific respect the title names: teacher-mediated recontextualization as an ecological achievement. The related-Lu programme is one programme, counted once. Designs are interview- and perception-heavy. There is no observation plus materials comparison of everyday recontextualization. C. Wang (2022) remains outstanding as a PDF; Iftikhar et al. (2024) remains landscape only until that PDF is read closely. It might be said that Pakistan CFL research already covers materials. It covers landscape and fit. It does not cover recontextualization as everyday practice.

## 2.5  Synthesis and Research Gap

The families have not been asked to answer one another for this population. This section does not recap 2.1–2.4 paper by paper.

### 2.5.1  What the Literature Collectively Establishes

Materials travel with mismatch; use matters more than product scores (Guerrettaz & Johnston, 2013; Harwood, 2021, 2026; Toledo-Sandoval, 2020; Dassanayake, 2023). Adaptation is professional and uneven; move-counts are not recontextualization (Rathert & Cabaroğlu, 2021, 2022; Hanifa et al., 2024; Criado, 2023). Ecological agency explains that unevenness in EFL textbook settings (Biesta & Tedder, 2007; Priestley et al., 2012; L. Wang, 2022; Yan & Weng, 2026; Dao et al., 2025). CFL localization is thinkable as teacher work and as multilingual mediation (Zhao et al., 2024b; Yasasri, 2024; Q. Zhang et al., 2020; D. Wang, 2022; Nan et al., 2023). Pakistan CFL writing names localization and recommends Urdu-enriched books (Azeem et al., 2022; Khan et al., 2022; Hanif, 2023; Lu et al., 2024; Lu & Hanif, 2025). Lu et al. (2024) and Lu and Hanif (2025) are related publications, not two independent confirmations of that conclusion.

| Conversation | Typical context | Typical method | What it cannot show |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ecological agency + prescribed textbooks | China / Vietnam EFL | Case study, often interviews + some observation | Pakistani teachers of Chinese |
| Recontextualization / adaptation of imported materials | Malaysia, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Australia CFL textbooks | Textbook analysis, teacher report, some observation | Agency and CFL practice joined in Pakistan |
| Chinese education in Pakistan | Universities, HSK use | Programme studies, perceptions of fit | Classroom recontextualization as production of localization |

### 2.5.2  The Intersection of Materials Recontextualization and Teacher Agency

What the families have not done is join recontextualization as the pedagogic mechanism with ecological agency as the achievement, for this population, with observation plus materials comparison.

Four near-misses should be named and not treated as filling the gap. Alvunger (2018) already joins agency and recontextualization, but in Swedish curriculum making. Nan et al. (2023) already speak to recontextualization of translanguaging in Indonesian Mandarin teaching; that is not ecological agency plus materials comparison among Pakistani teachers of Chinese. Yasasri (2024), Dassanayake (2023), Ling et al. (2023), and the Zhao programme already speak to CFL localization or contextualization, but not in Pakistan, and not as observation plus materials comparison in that setting. Loo (2026) already treats localization as teacher-mediated work with borrowed curricula; the language object is not Chinese in Pakistan. Ecological textbook-agency studies already include observation (Yan & Weng, 2026; Dao et al., 2025; Guerrettaz & Johnston, 2013); the method exists, the population and the material object do not.

### 2.5.3  Conceptual, Empirical and Methodological Limitations

The limits have already been named in 2.1.4, 2.2.5, 2.3.4, and 2.4.6. They synthesize as three warrants rather than as a second tour of those subsections.

If two research conversations have not been joined, a study that joins them can be justified — provided the join is not already done under another name. Alvunger and Nan occupy parts of the join in other places; they do not cancel the Pakistan CFL problem. If a practice is claimed to happen in classrooms, interview-only or textbook-only studies are incomplete evidence of that practice. Findings from EFL or from Chinese teaching in Australia, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, or China are analogues, not substitutes, for Pakistani teachers of Chinese.

### 2.5.4  The Contextual Gap in Pakistani Universities

Population D has landscape and perceived fit, not enactment. The product logic of new or Urdu-enriched books is what this review contests. The classroom is the missing site: where localization is produced, restricted, or redirected under nested conditions.

### 2.5.5  Resulting Research Problem and Connection to the Research Questions

Previous studies have separately examined ecological teacher agency in the use of prescribed language textbooks, chiefly among EFL teachers in China, Vietnam and related curriculum-reform settings, and the adaptation or recontextualization of imported and global teaching materials, including ELT cases in Malaysia, Indonesia and rural Southeast Asia and CFL textbook or contextualization studies in Sri Lanka, Australia and related sites. However, limited empirical research has investigated recontextualization of Chinese-language teaching materials and ecological teacher agency in a single design, and Pakistani teachers of Chinese are almost absent from both literatures. In particular, insufficient attention has been given to classroom observation plus materials comparison that would show how localization is produced in everyday teaching rather than received as textbook production, and to the institutional, pedagogical and professional conditions that shape what Pakistani teachers of Chinese adapt, how they adapt it, and why.

The research questions follow as the destination of the review, not as a new list:

1. What recontextualization practices do Pakistani teachers of Chinese enact when using Chinese-language teaching materials (addition, omission, substitution, modification, reordering, supplementation, translanguaging, cultural filtering, or faithful transmission)?
2. How do teachers justify these decisions in relation to learners, materials, pedagogy, culture, language resources, assessment, time, and professional identity?
3. In what ways is teacher agency achieved, restricted, or redirected across the iterational, projective, and practical-evaluative dimensions of ecological agency?
4. Which nested conditions—classroom, collegial and institutional, examination and HSK policy, professional community, and wider sociocultural setting—enable or constrain teacher-mediated localization?

Chapter 3 turns the gap into a working model: recontextualization as the mechanism, ecological agency as the achievement, materials-in-use as the empirical window. The study joins Families A and B in population D, with observation plus materials comparison.
