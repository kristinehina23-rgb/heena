## 2.1  Introduction: context, problem, response

Researchers of language education already know a good deal about four neighbouring issues. They know that textbooks do not simply enter classrooms as finished scripts: teachers omit, add, substitute, reorder, and supplement, and the book often becomes the de facto curriculum (Guerrettaz & Johnston, 2013; Rathert & Cabaroğlu, 2022; Guerrettaz et al., 2022; Harwood, 2021, 2026). They know that whether teachers can make those moves is not only a matter of belief or skill: ecological accounts treat agency as something achieved in conditions of possibility and constraint (Biesta & Tedder, 2007; Priestley et al., 2012; Biesta, Priestley, & Robinson, 2015). They know that teachers of Chinese as a foreign or additional language mediate materials, culture, and multilingual repertoires, including by drawing local life into Chinese lessons (Bao et al., 2020; Ling et al., 2023; Yasasri, 2024; Han, 2022). And they know that Chinese-language programmes in Pakistan have grown, that HSK-oriented materials are widely used, and that “localization” is a recurring policy and academic word (C. Wang, 2022; Lu et al., 2024; Lu & Hanif, 2025; Naheed, 2026).

That context would seem to leave little room for another study of materials. The conceptual problem is that these four conversations have not been made to answer the question this thesis asks.

Previous studies have separately examined ecological teacher agency in the use of prescribed language textbooks, chiefly among EFL teachers in China, Vietnam and related curriculum-reform settings, and the adaptation or recontextualization of imported and global teaching materials, including ELT cases in Malaysia, Indonesia and rural Southeast Asia and CFL textbook or contextualization studies in Sri Lanka, Australia and related sites. However, limited empirical research has investigated recontextualization of Chinese-language teaching materials and ecological teacher agency in a single design, and Pakistani teachers of Chinese are almost absent from both literatures. In particular, insufficient attention has been given to classroom observation plus materials comparison that would show how localization is produced in everyday teaching rather than received as textbook production, and to the institutional, pedagogical and professional conditions that shape what Pakistani teachers of Chinese adapt, how they adapt it, and why.

The claim of this chapter is therefore smaller than the claim of the thesis. It is a claim about the research conversation:

> Previous research has examined ecological teacher agency in prescribed-textbook settings and the recontextualization of imported language materials as largely separate conversations; neither conversation has produced an empirically grounded account of how Pakistani teachers of Chinese recontextualize Chinese-language teaching materials in class.

If that claim holds, a qualitative study from an ecological perspective is not a relabelling of “materials fit.” It is a warranted next move in the conversation (Booth et al., 2024, chs. 2, 5–6).

The chapter supports the claim with four reasons. Section 2.2 shows that materials research has shifted from evaluating books as products to studying how teachers use and transform them, so localization cannot be treated only as textbook design. Section 2.3 shows that ecological agency explains why teachers can or cannot change materials, but that explanation has been built mainly in EFL reform settings. Section 2.4 shows that CFL/CAL studies already describe teacher mediation of Chinese materials, language, and culture, yet rarely join that work to the ecological model as the main frame, and not in Pakistan. Section 2.5 shows that research on Chinese-language education in Pakistan describes programmes, materials, and perceived fit, not teacher-mediated recontextualization as an ecological achievement. Section 2.6 acknowledges the strongest near-misses. Section 2.7 restates the gap as the destination of the review.

The search behind this chapter is an Undermind deep search of work on teachers’ use and adaptation of materials and on teacher agency in curriculum and materials, plus CFL/CAL studies. The evidence tables cover papers 1–220 of a stated 240; the last file contains forty rows, not sixty. English-language journals dominate. That scope is enough to make the problem visible. It is not enough to claim that no Chinese-language paper exists. CNKI remains part of the argument, not an afterthought.

## 2.2  Reason one: localization is a problem of use, not only of textbook production

Materials research has moved the object of inquiry from the textbook as a finished product to the textbook as something teachers do things with. If that shift is taken seriously, a thesis that only evaluates how well HSK materials “fit” Pakistan would be answering yesterday’s question.

### 2.2.1  From content analysis to consumption and production

Harwood’s research agenda insists that materials be studied as content, as consumption, and as production: not only what is on the page, but how teachers and learners use, adapt, create, and subvert resources (Harwood, 2021, 2026). Guerrettaz and colleagues similarly argue that materials-use research must attend to pedagogy, classroom interaction, culture, language diversity, and power, rather than remaining a branch of textbook evaluation (Guerrettaz et al., 2022). The warrant is straightforward. If the educational consequences of a book depend on what is done with it, then evaluating the book in isolation cannot explain localization.

This does not make content analysis useless. It relocates it. Textbook analysis can show what was available to be recontextualized. It cannot show what was recontextualized.

### 2.2.2  Materials in the classroom ecology

Guerrettaz and Johnston’s classroom study remains the clearest demonstration that a textbook can become the de facto curriculum and structure interaction, not merely supply topics (Guerrettaz & Johnston, 2013). Later sociomaterial and ecological work treats materials as affordances, perturbations, and interactional resources: they shape what can be said and done, and they are themselves reshaped in use (Z. Li, 2020; D. Li, 2023; Li & Xu, 2020; Matsumoto, 2019; Kim, 2023). Li and Li’s materials-use arc is useful here because it makes episodic classroom use visible rather than treating “using the book” as a single act (Li & Li, 2021).

For Pakistani Chinese classrooms, this literature supplies a method of seeing. It does not yet supply the cases. The HSK-oriented book may organise the lesson; it may also be pushed to the margin by worksheets, oral translation, exam drills, or local examples. That is an empirical question. It is not answered by describing the book’s table of contents.

### 2.2.3  Adaptation as professional practice

A second strand treats adaptation as principled professional work. Rathert and Cabaroğlu distinguish coursebook-led use from teacher-led adaptation and offer a usable set of moves: omission, addition, substitution, modification (Rathert & Cabaroğlu, 2021, 2022). Hanifa and colleagues, and Mohamad Uri and Abdul Aziz, show those moves in imported-textbook settings under time, training, and examination pressure (Hanifa et al., 2024; Hanifa & Yusra, 2023; Mohamad Uri & Abdul Aziz, 2024). Song’s evaluation–adaptation–exploitation cycle warns that following the book can be a professional stance, not only a failure of imagination (Song, 2024). Li, Gao, and Cui, and Bouckaert, go further: teachers are users, analysts, and designers, and materials work is a site of professional learning (Li et al., 2021; Bouckaert, 2019).

Two qualifications keep this strand from swallowing the thesis. First, frequent adaptation is not the same as localization. Criado found teachers changing a large share of activities while remaining largely faithful to textbook sequence; procedural change even made some lessons more teacher-fronted (Criado, 2023). A count of additions and deletions in Pakistani Chinese classes could therefore look “active” and still leave the HSK course as the curriculum. Second, teachers’ reported willingness to adapt often exceeds what they do. Restricted or “addition-only” agency under exams, time, and institutional pressure is a recurring finding in full-text ecological studies of prescribed textbooks (Yan & Weng, 2026; Tao & Weng, 2025; Neupane, 2025). Interview-only designs will reproduce that gap as if it were the finding.

### 2.2.4  Recontextualization names the pedagogic move better than “add local colour”

“Localization” in language-materials research is an unstable word. Tian and Weninger’s review of local English textbooks shows that “the local” is constructed in several, sometimes contradictory, ways, and that the research is geographically uneven (Tian & Weninger, 2026). Teachers add local references even when books already claim to represent local culture (Toledo-Sandoval, 2020). They may also filter or censor cultural content (Gray, 2000).

Recontextualization is the more precise term for this thesis because it names a pedagogic operation: selection, relocation, silencing, and transformation when knowledge and texts are moved into a new classroom (see also Yu, 2015, on policy recontextualization; Alvunger, 2018, on curriculum recontextualization). Yasasri’s analysis of global Chinese textbooks used in Sri Lanka argues that such books can marginalize local cultures and L1 resources (Yasasri, 2024). Ling, Han, and Ma show CFL teachers in Australia building lessons from students’ sociocultural contexts rather than only from the book’s China (Ling et al., 2023). Loo’s Southeast Asian study is especially useful as a warrant for looking at teachers rather than at new national books: localization was largely left to teachers, with little institutional support, and was reactive, fragmented, and politically shaped (Loo, 2026).

What this strand cannot do is substitute Sri Lanka, Australia, or Southeast Asia for Pakistan. It can show that the field already knows localization is more than producing a local edition. The thesis takes that as a starting point, not as a finding.

### 2.2.5  What reason one contributes

Reason one supports the chapter claim in this way: if the serious literature on materials has already left textbook evaluation behind, then a Pakistani study that only diagnoses misfit in HSK *Standard Course* and recommends a national or Urdu-medium book would be repeating a product logic the field has outgrown. The open question is how teachers recontextualize Chinese materials in class, under what conditions, and with what space for manoeuvre.

## 2.3  Reason two: ecological agency explains materials decisions—but not yet for Pakistani teachers of Chinese

Even a precise vocabulary of adaptation moves cannot explain why one teacher adds local examples and another does not, or why a teacher who believes in localization only “does addition.” Ecological teacher agency was developed to answer that kind of question. Its empirical base, however, is not Pakistani CFL.

### 2.3.1  Agency as achievement, not a trait

Biesta and Tedder’s ecological account treats agency as something achieved in the interplay of routine, purpose, judgement, resources, and contexts-for-action, not as a property a teacher possesses (Biesta & Tedder, 2007). Priestley and colleagues develop that account for curriculum making: agency varies with beliefs *and* with environmental conditions of possibility and constraint; it has a temporal shape—iterational (past), projective (future), practical-evaluative (present); and it requires more than individual conviction, including professional discourse and supportive institutional cultures (Priestley et al., 2012, 2016; Biesta, Priestley, & Robinson, 2015). Leijen, Pedaste, and Lepp connect that model to reflection as a way of strengthening purpose and the capacity to adapt curriculum (Leijen et al., 2020). Hardman has recently pressed for still clearer distinctions among ecological, relational, and new-materialist uses of “agency,” a reminder not to treat the word as a slogan (Hardman, 2025).

The warrant for this thesis is the definition itself. If agency is achieved, then “Pakistani teachers lack agency” is the wrong kind of sentence. The right questions are: in which conditions is agency achieved, restricted, or redirected, and what recontextualization practices are its visible traces?

### 2.3.2  Agency when the textbook is prescribed

A dense EFL literature now applies that model to new or prescribed textbooks. Yan and Weng show Chinese high-school English teachers selectively adapting a new series through interactions among teacher, student, and context—and the title phrase “we can only do addition” names a restricted form of agency that is highly relevant to HSK-led courses (Yan & Weng, 2026). Dao, Ngo, and Chi offer a usable coding of acceptance, adaptation, and resistance among Vietnamese teachers implementing new textbooks (Dao et al., 2025). Chen and Shu distinguish approval, compliant agency, and persistent agency across classroom and institutional settings (Chen & Shu, 2026). Tao and Weng describe “restricted agency” among teachers who support reform ideologically but remain shackled in practice (Tao & Weng, 2025). L. Wang’s ecological study of Chinese EFL teachers distinguishes constrained, transformative, and progressive agency and shows that reform-oriented beliefs do not guarantee agency: school culture, exams, colleagues, and support shape what can be done (L. Wang, 2022). Tran, Truong, and related Vietnamese work trace the same temporal triad in new-textbook implementation (Tran, 2022; Truong, 2023).

This is the closest empirical family to the present study. It is still the wrong subject and, usually, the wrong language. These teachers are typically English teachers under national English-curriculum reform. Pakistani teachers of Chinese work with a different official object (often HSK-oriented Chinese materials), a different professional status, and a different multilingual ecology. Analogues can guide coding. They cannot stand in for the cases.

### 2.3.3  Nested conditions, not “context” as residual

Namgung, Moate, and Ruohotie-Lyhty map teacher agency onto nested ecosystems—micro, meso, exo, macro, chrono—which gives a more disciplined way to talk about “context” than a leftover list of constraints (Namgung et al., 2023). Chen’s Bronfenbrenner-based study of primary EFL teachers in South China similarly shows agency emerging through relations with students, colleagues, parents, leaders, and policy (Chen, 2023). Tao’s work on Russian-language teachers in China is a useful caution for university settings: the university, not only the nation-state, may be the strongest filter of policy (Tao, 2021). Priestley and colleagues’ distinction between input and output regulation, and their account of creative mediation versus strategic compliance, will matter wherever HSK scores, university examinations, or inspection organise Chinese teaching (Priestley et al., 2016). Time, syllabus, supervision, and student realities appear again and again as practical-evaluative conditions (Rivera Cuayahuitl & Pérez Carranza, 2015; Huang & Guo, 2024; Neupane, 2025).

For this thesis, those layers are not background colour. They are part of the object: the ecological conditions that shape what Pakistani teachers of Chinese adapt, how, and why.

### 2.3.4  Beliefs and identity are resources, not the whole story

Biesta, Priestley, and Robinson insist that beliefs matter as cultural resources for agency but do not suffice without collective professional discourse and supportive cultures (Biesta, Priestley, & Robinson, 2015). Studies of identity reconstruction through new textbooks, of identity–agency negotiation during reform, and of teachers’ development as materials designers all show that materials work changes who teachers can be, and that who they have been shapes what they dare to change (J. Zhang et al., 2025; Liyanage & Walker, 2023; Li & Gao, 2025). Bao, Ren, and Wang’s study of Chinese teachers in Belarus already brings identity, community, and materials use together in CAL—an important CFL-adjacent finding, but not an ecological study of Pakistan (Bao et al., 2020).

### 2.3.5  What reason two contributes

Reason two supports the chapter claim in this way: the field already has a rigorous language for teacher agency in textbook use. That language has been tested mainly on EFL reform. Using it among Pakistani teachers of Chinese is not a theoretical invention. It is an empirical extension that the conversation has not made.

## 2.4  Reason three: CFL/CAL research shows mediation—but not teacher-mediated localization in Pakistan

If Sections 2.2 and 2.3 were enough, this thesis could be an ELT study with Chinese examples swapped in. It cannot. The title is about Chinese-language teaching materials and Pakistani teachers of Chinese. The CFL/CAL literature must therefore do real work: it shows that Chinese teaching already involves recontextualization and multilingual mediation. It does not show that work as ecological agency in Pakistan.

### 2.4.1  Chinese teachers as agents with materials

Bao, Ren, and Wang argue that CAL teachers’ materials use is mediated by beliefs, identities, and community relationships (Bao et al., 2020). Han documents Chinese teachers in Norway rebuilding materials under curricular openness, limited resources, and uncertain assessment (Han, 2026). Wang and Bale show new K–12 Chinese teachers in the United States creating curriculum with incomplete preparation (Wang & Bale, 2024). S. Zhang and colleagues show CAL teachers’ ambivalent views of translanguaging inside an activity system of tools, rules, community, and division of labour (S. Zhang et al., 2025). Geng and Lu’s kit-bag study of Chinese teachers in Latin America treats handmade and everyday objects as identity mediators in resource-constrained settings (Geng & Lu, 2026).

These studies establish that Chinese teachers outside China are not mere deliverers of imported books. None of them is a study of Pakistani university (or institute) teachers of Chinese. The professional ecology—Confucius Institute partnerships, HSK, local contract conditions, Urdu and English as mediating languages—cannot be read off Belarus, Norway, or the United States.

### 2.4.2  Recontextualizing Chinese materials and local life

Yasasri’s corpus study of global Chinese textbooks in Sri Lanka is the most explicit CFL use of “recontextualizing learning material”: the books, on that analysis, sideline local cultures and L1 resources (Yasasri, 2024). Dassanayake’s Sri Lankan checklist for localized Chinese textbooks likewise starts from global books’ neglect of L1, local culture, translation, cost, and regional need (Dassanayake, 2023). Both are valuable—and both are still closer to product evaluation than to classroom agency. Ling, Han, and Ma, and Zhao, show a different move: CFL teachers in Australia turning students’ everyday practices and funds of knowledge into Chinese-learning content (Ling et al., 2023; Zhao, 2020, 2024). Duangmanee and Waluyo’s Thai school narratives show institutions trying to localize Chinese teaching while remaining dependent on external Chinese-language expertise (Duangmanee & Waluyo, 2024).

The pattern is consistent. CFL localization is thinkable as teacher work and as textbook revision. The teacher-work side is documented in Australia, Thailand, Indonesia, and related sites. Pakistan is not in that set.

### 2.4.3  Translanguaging as recontextualization—without reducing localization to Urdu glosses

A substantial CAL/CFL literature now treats teachers’ and students’ bilingual repertoires as instructional resources rather than as interference (Han, 2022; Zheng, 2019; Q. Zhang et al., 2020; Tian, 2021; Huang et al., 2025). Indonesian Mandarin teaching is an especially close analogue for multilingual South Asian classrooms: Puspitasari, Yannuar, and Zubaidi describe CFL lecturers using Chinese, English, Indonesian, Pinyin, translations, and visuals to close comprehension gaps (Puspitasari et al., 2024); Nan and colleagues explicitly frame “recontextualization of translanguaging” in Indonesian Mandarin teaching (Nan et al., 2023). D. Wang’s Hong Kong work treats translanguaging as a social-justice strategy for ethnic-minority students under monolingual pressure (D. Wang, 2022). Liu warns that “translanguaging” can also reinforce English dominance when testing and materials pull that way (Liu, 2025). Yuan and Lee distinguish transitional translation from a deeper transformative understanding of translanguaging in Mandarin immersion (Yuan & Lee, 2024).

For Pakistan, the implication is double. Urdu, English, regional languages, and Chinese are likely ecological resources in the classroom. Localization cannot be defined as printing Urdu glosses in a new edition. At the same time, multilingual practice is not automatically just or localizing. It can be managerial, transitional, or even hierarchy-preserving. That is why observation plus materials comparison is required: language choice on a page and language choice in a lesson are not the same evidence.

### 2.4.4  What reason three contributes

Reason three supports the chapter claim in this way: a Chinese-language education thesis has a CFL conversation to join, not only an ELT one. That conversation already knows teachers recontextualize Chinese materials and languages. It has not joined that knowledge to ecological agency among Pakistani teachers of Chinese.

## 2.5  Reason four: Pakistan CFL research describes the landscape of fit, not the production of localization

The fourth reason is the simplest and the one most easily missed, because the writer already “has Pakistan” in earlier drafts. Those drafts asked how well materials fit Pakistani universities. That is a different problem.

### 2.5.1  What is known about Chinese teaching in Pakistan

Chinese-language education in Pakistan has a documented programme history, institutional variety, and a materials landscape in which HSK-oriented series, especially *HSK Standard Course*, are widely used. C. Wang’s (2022) doctoral dissertation maps Pakistani Chinese teaching as an international-communication and programme system rather than as classroom recontextualization of a particular book. Naheed’s mixed-methods survey of 55 local Chinese-language teachers reports that 78.18% used *HSK Standard Course* as their main instructional resource and recommends Urdu-enriched textbooks as a localization response (Naheed, 2026).

Two closely related publications from the same Guangdong University of Foreign Studies group examine *HSK Standard Course* in Pakistan and must **not** be counted as independent evidence. Lu et al. (2024) combine a literature review, student questionnaires, and interviews with Pakistani Chinese teachers, and they recommend Urdu annotations, cultural comparison, and localized modules. Lu and Hanif (2025) is a follow-on article—same first author, same corresponding author (Hanif), same textbook object—based on document analysis and further teacher interviews. It cites Lu et al. (2024) as a source and turns the emphasis toward pedagogical challenges and Urdu as a support language. The two papers share a research programme, overlapping authorship, and the same HSK materials problem. They describe perceived fit and propose materials-production remedies. They do not replace classroom observation plus materials comparison.

Those sources are indispensable as **background**. They are not the theory of this thesis and not its finding. They show that localization is already talked about as a materials-production remedy. They do not show how teachers recontextualize the books they already have.

### 2.5.2  The empty column in the international conversation

The Undermind tables that ground Sections 2.2–2.4 are themselves evidence. Across 220 ranked papers on teacher agency, materials use, adaptation, and CFL/CAL mediation, the context column fills with China, Vietnam, Malaysia, Indonesia, Australia, Sri Lanka, Belarus, Norway, Hong Kong, Nepal, Thailand, and related sites. It does not fill with Pakistani teachers of Chinese. That absence is not a search accident to be patched by one more keyword. It is the geographic face of the chapter claim.

### 2.5.3  Analogues, not substitutes

The closest comparative cases remain analogues. South and Southeast Asian teachers adapting imported English books under exam and workload pressure (Hanifa et al., 2024; Mohamad Uri & Abdul Aziz, 2024; Neupane, 2025; Thapa & Upadhayaya, 2026) can inform interview questions about time, tests, and “local culture.” Chinese teachers outside China (Bao et al., 2020; Han, 2026; Ling et al., 2023) can inform questions about professional identity and local content. EFL teachers under prescribed textbooks (Yan & Weng, 2026; Dao et al., 2025) can inform coding of restricted agency. None of them is a Pakistani teacher of Chinese working with Chinese-language materials. Booth’s warrant here must be stated, not implied: analogue evidence can motivate a study; it cannot replace it (Booth et al., 2024, chs. 7–8).

### 2.5.4  What reason four contributes

Reason four supports the chapter claim in this way: Pakistan is not an unstudied country in CFL. It is under-studied in the specific respect the title names—teacher-mediated recontextualization as an ecological achievement. Filling that respect is not the same as writing a longer version of a fit evaluation.

## 2.6  Acknowledgments and responses

A fair argument names the readings that come closest to closing the gap (Booth et al., 2024, ch. 9).

*It might be said that* agency and recontextualization have already been joined. Alvunger studies teachers’ curriculum agency with Bernstein’s pedagogic device and shows teachers borrowing and combining content across spaces (Alvunger, 2018). **Response:** that join is real, and it supports the framework in Chapter 3. The setting is Swedish curriculum making, not Chinese-language teaching in Pakistan. Framework near-misses do not fill empirical gaps.

*It might be said that* CFL recontextualization is already studied. Yasasri (2024), Dassanayake (2023), Nan et al. (2023), and Ling et al. (2023) all speak to localizing or recontextualizing Chinese materials or multilingual practice. **Response:** granted. Two of these are primarily textbook or checklist studies; Nan’s “recontextualization” is of translanguaging in Indonesian Mandarin teaching; Ling et al. are Australian classroom teachers. None offers ecological agency among Pakistani teachers of Chinese, and none is designed as classroom observation plus materials comparison in that setting.

*It might be said that* ecological textbook-agency studies already include observation. Many do (Yan & Weng, 2026; Dao et al., 2025; Guerrettaz & Johnston, 2013). **Response:** the method exists. The population and the material object do not. Importing the method is the point of Chapter 4, not a reason to skip the study.

*It might be said that* Pakistan CFL research already covers materials. **Response:** it covers landscape and perceived fit, and it often concludes toward new or Urdu-enriched books (Lu et al., 2024; Lu & Hanif, 2025; Naheed, 2026). Lu et al. (2024) and Lu and Hanif (2025) are related publications, not two independent confirmations of that conclusion. That product logic is exactly what this review is contesting.

## 2.7  The gap (destination of the review)

The four reasons converge on the same paragraph. It is restated here so that Chapter 3 has to operationalize it rather than invent a new problem.

Previous studies have separately examined ecological teacher agency in the use of prescribed language textbooks, chiefly among EFL teachers in China, Vietnam and related curriculum-reform settings, and the adaptation or recontextualization of imported and global teaching materials, including ELT cases in Malaysia, Indonesia and rural Southeast Asia and CFL textbook or contextualization studies in Sri Lanka, Australia and related sites. However, limited empirical research has investigated recontextualization of Chinese-language teaching materials and ecological teacher agency in a single design, and Pakistani teachers of Chinese are almost absent from both literatures. In particular, insufficient attention has been given to classroom observation plus materials comparison that would show how localization is produced in everyday teaching rather than received as textbook production, and to the institutional, pedagogical and professional conditions that shape what Pakistani teachers of Chinese adapt, how they adapt it, and why.

That gap is conceptual and practical at once (Booth et al., 2024, ch. 2). Conceptually, we lack an account of localization as teacher-mediated recontextualization in this setting. Practically, teacher education and institutional policy that aim at “a local textbook” may be aiming at the wrong site of change if localization is already being produced—or blocked—in classrooms.

## 2.8  Chapter summary

| Reason | What the conversation offers | What it cannot answer |
|---|---|---|
| 2.2 Materials-in-use and recontextualization | Moves, classroom ecology, a shift away from product evaluation | How Pakistani teachers of Chinese actually recontextualize Chinese materials |
| 2.3 Ecological agency | Agency as achievement; restricted/compliant/transformative forms; nested conditions | Those forms among Pakistani Chinese teachers in HSK-shaped institutions |
| 2.4 CFL/CAL mediation | Identity, local content, translanguaging in Chinese teaching | Ecological agency in Pakistan; observation plus materials comparison there |
| 2.5 Pakistan CFL | Programme landscape; HSK use; talk of localization as new books (including related Lu publications) | Classroom production of localization |

The research questions of the thesis follow as the review’s destination, not as a new list:

1. What recontextualization practices do Pakistani teachers of Chinese enact when using Chinese-language teaching materials?  
2. How do they justify those decisions?  
3. In what ways is teacher agency achieved, restricted, or redirected across iterational, projective, and practical-evaluative dimensions?  
4. Which nested conditions enable or constrain teacher-mediated localization?

Chapter 3 turns the gap into a working model: recontextualization as the mechanism, ecological agency as the achievement, materials-in-use as the empirical window.
