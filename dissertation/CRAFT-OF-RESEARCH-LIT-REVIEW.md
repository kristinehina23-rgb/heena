## 5. Warrants you must not hide (ch. 8)

A warrant is the principle that lets a reason support a claim. State these once, plainly:

1. If two research conversations have not been joined, a study that joins them can be justified — **provided** the join is not already done under another name. (Test this warrant against Alvunger and Nan. They join *parts* of the story in *other* places. They do not cancel the Pakistan CFL problem.)  
2. If a practice is claimed to happen in classrooms, interview-only or textbook-only studies are incomplete evidence of that practice.  
3. Findings from EFL or from Chinese teaching in Australia, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, or China are analogues, not substitutes, for Pakistani teachers of Chinese.

If a paragraph only “reviews” a paper without using one of these warrants, it is a dump.

## 6. Acknowledgments and responses (ch. 9)

Booth: good arguments name the best objections.

Build a short **9.x** move at the end of each main section, and a fuller one at the end of 2.5:

- *It might be said that* localization of Chinese materials is already studied (Yasasri, Dassanayake, Ling).  
  **Response:** those studies analyse textbooks, checklists, or contextualization outside Pakistan; they do not show ecological agency among Pakistani teachers of Chinese.  
- *It might be said that* ecological agency in textbook use is already studied (Yan & Weng, Dao, Chen & Shu).  
  **Response:** those teachers are mostly EFL teachers under national English reforms, not local Chinese teachers working with HSK-type materials.  
- *It might be said that* Alvunger already joins agency and recontextualization.  
  **Response:** yes, in Swedish curriculum making. That supports your *framework*, not your *empirical* gap.  
- *It might be said that* Pakistan CFL research already covers materials.  
  **Response:** it covers landscape and fit. It does not cover recontextualization as everyday practice.

Use Booth’s vocabulary of acknowledgment (9.6): *although, granted, it is true that, nevertheless, even so.*

## 8. Paragraph recipe (chs. 11.5, 12, 15)

One paragraph, one step in the argument.

1. **Topic sentence = your claim** about the literature (no citation yet).  
2. **Evidence:** two to five sources **in conversation** (who agrees, who differs, what method limits them).  
3. **Relevance sentence** (12.6): tell the reader what this group of sources does for *your* problem.  
4. Prefer **paraphrase** of findings; **quote** only a term of art (*agency as achievement*, *materials-in-use*, *space for manoeuvre*) (12.1–12.5).  
5. Style (ch. 15): old information before new; characters as subjects (“Teachers in Yan and Weng’s study…” not “It was found that…”); complexity last.

### Worked paragraph (model only; verify against PDFs before using)

> Materials research has moved the object of inquiry from the textbook as a finished product to the textbook as something teachers do things with. Guerrettaz and Johnston show how a coursebook can become the de facto curriculum and organise classroom interaction; Rathert and Cabaroğlu treat adaptation (omission, addition, substitution, modification) as a professional response to mismatch, not as misuse; Harwood argues that materials research must include consumption and production, not only content analysis. Even studies of frequent adaptation, however, warn that changing activities does not by itself amount to localization: Criado found teachers altering a large share of tasks while remaining faithful to textbook sequence. That pattern matters for Pakistani Chinese teaching because a count of “adaptations” would still miss whether teachers are recontextualizing materials for local learners or merely rearranging a prescribed HSK course. The literature can describe the moves. It has not yet shown those moves as ecological agency among Pakistani teachers of Chinese.

Test: delete the last two sentences. If the paragraph still looks like a summary of famous names, it has failed Booth’s 12.6 (showing how evidence is relevant).

## 13. Dissertation register (write as a researcher, not as a note-taker)

Chapter 2 is a chapter of a PhD thesis in international Chinese-language education. Write it that way.

- Do not put “working draft,” Booth, Undermind, Zotero, or file paths in the chapter body. Scope and CNKI belong in one calm paragraph.
- Do not number “competing definitions” with Strength/Limit bullets. Narrate the debate, then state the working definition.
- Do not use four-level headings in Chapter 2. Locked third-level heads are in `OUTLINE.md`. “Origins and Definitions” is a few paragraphs, not a history. Applications / debates / significance are still paragraphs, not extra heads.
- Do not write agency as a driver or localization as a pre-set goal.
- Cite researchers as interlocutors: what they claimed, on what evidence, with what limit for *this* problem.
- Keep Chinese terms (再语境化, 教师能动性, 本土化) where they earn precision; do not sprinkle them.
- Analogues motivate; they do not substitute. Related publications (Lu et al., 2024; Lu & Hanif, 2025) are one programme, not two confirmations.
- Observation plus materials comparison remains non-negotiable for claims about classroom practice.
