## 1.1  Research Background

In 2005, Pakistan’s first Confucius Institute opened at the National University of Modern Languages in Islamabad. At that point, Chinese was a language studied by a small number of people, mostly connected to diplomacy or specialized trade. Within a decade, that picture changed. Four more Confucius Institutes followed, at the University of the Punjab, the University of Karachi, the University of Agriculture Faisalabad, and the University of Sargodha, alongside a growing network of vocational and short-course centres (Hanif, 2023). This expansion did not happen on its own. It tracked the deepening of Pakistan–China relations, and later the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor, which tied Chinese-language ability directly to jobs, scholarships, and mobility for a generation of Pakistani students (Iftikhar et al., 2024; Khan et al., 2022). By the time Hanif (2023) documented the system in detail, demand for Chinese had reached a level where universities and technical institutes across the country offered it through degree programmes, required courses, electives, and short courses, each with its own learners, its own goals, and its own level of institutional support.

Growth on this scale created a system, but not a uniform one. At a public engineering university studied by Khan et al. (2022), students and policymakers agreed that mandatory Chinese classes existed to secure jobs and scholarships, but their accounts of the classroom itself, class size, and language of instruction diverged sharply once implementation was examined closely. This unevenness sits inside a still larger picture. Pakistan is already a multilingual country where English, Urdu, and regional languages carry unequal social and educational weight (Ali & David, 2022). Chinese entered this landscape as one more language competing for classroom time and institutional attention, not as a subject taught in isolation.

Students who moved through this expanding system reported real difficulty. University learners of Chinese in Pakistan describe persistent worry about speaking, reading, and writing the language, and doubt over whether they will ever reach a usable level of proficiency (Aftab & Abbasi, 2024; Iftikhar et al., 2024). Read on their own, these reports could suggest that Chinese is simply a hard language for Pakistani learners. Read alongside what is known about the curriculum, they suggest something more specific. Difficulty is not free-floating. It comes through a particular set of teaching materials, a particular sequence of content, a particular choice of classroom language, and a particular amount of support built into lessons. So the next question research had to ask was not whether learners struggled, but what exactly they were struggling with.

That question led directly to teaching materials. Several studies traced learner difficulty back to a reliance on textbooks and resources designed for international or exam-oriented markets, materials that assume a learner background very different from a Pakistani one (Azeem et al., 2022; Hanif, 2023; Lu et al., 2024; Naheed, 2026). These materials were found to carry limited cultural familiarity for Pakistani students, to explain new content in ways that did not connect to Urdu or English, and to offer little built-in support for teachers working with large, mixed-ability classes. Studies working directly with Pakistani teachers and learners recommended a fairly consistent set of fixes: Urdu annotations, locally relevant cultural comparisons, revised modules, and stronger teacher preparation (Lu et al., 2024; Lu & Hanif, 2025). Lu and Hanif (2025) is a follow-on article from the same programme as Lu et al. (2024), not a second independent confirmation. At this point, the research literature had moved from documenting a problem to proposing solutions.

But the solutions proposed so far share one feature. They are all aimed at producing something new: a localized textbook, a revised module, a training programme. None of them ask what is happening right now, in the classes already running with the materials already in place. A localized textbook can take years to reach a classroom. In the meantime, teachers still have to walk in and teach. Faced with a textbook that does not quite fit, a teacher has to do something with it in the moment, whether that means translating a passage into Urdu, adding a locally familiar example, skipping a section that will not land, or simply teaching the page as written because there is no time or institutional room to do otherwise. This is where the present study begins, not with the materials that could exist in future, but with what teachers already do with the materials sitting in front of them.

Research from outside Pakistan gives a name and a shape to this kind of teacher activity. Guerrettaz and Johnston (2013) showed that a textbook can function as the de facto curriculum of a course, shaping not just content but the pattern of classroom interaction itself, and that this only becomes visible once materials are studied in use rather than as printed pages. Later work built on this by showing that what a textbook does in practice depends on what teachers and learners actually do with it: checking sections against their own judgement, choosing what to keep, skipping what will not work, adding to it, reordering it, improvising around it (Guerrettaz et al., 2022; Li & Xu, 2020; Rathert & Cabaroğlu, 2021, 2022). Even textbooks written specifically for a local market can still need this kind of adjustment, because the local context an author imagines while writing is rarely the exact context a particular class is sitting in (Toledo-Sandoval, 2020). Put simply, materials use is not a fixed property of the textbook. It is something teachers build, lesson by lesson.

This is the point where the present study introduces a working term: materials recontextualization, meaning the process through which teachers reinterpret and reshape published materials once those materials reach the classroom. This includes visible moves like translation, added explanation, cutting, substitution, and reordering, but also quieter shifts in emphasis, purpose, and how much room students get to participate. Localization is one possible outcome of this process, when a teacher’s changes happen to bring the material closer to Pakistani learners and their context. The two are related but not identical. A teacher might recontextualize material purely to finish a syllabus on time or manage a difficult exam schedule, with no localizing intention at all. Whether a given change counts as localization depends on whether it orients the material toward Pakistani learners, languages, cultures, institutions, or educational purposes, not merely on the fact that something on the page was changed, and not on whether the lesson was “better.”

How much a teacher can actually do with a set of materials is not simply a matter of skill or willingness. Ecological accounts of agency treat it as something achieved through a person’s relationship with their own past experience, their sense of future goals, and their judgement in the present moment, all in interaction with the people and resources around them (Biesta & Tedder, 2007; Priestley et al., 2012). Under this view, a teacher who adds an Urdu gloss to a difficult passage is not simply exercising a personal talent. That small act becomes possible because of what the teacher already knows, how much flexibility the institution allows, how the syllabus is assessed, and how much time is available to prepare. The same teacher might stay very close to the textbook in a different class, not from a lack of ability, but because the institutional conditions there leave no space to do otherwise. An ecological lens is what makes it possible to explain both kinds of decision within the same framework, instead of treating one as agency and the other as its absence.

Taken together, this is where the story so far leads. Research on Pakistan has documented the growth of Chinese-language education, the multilingual environment surrounding it, the difficulties learners report, and the shortcomings of current materials, and it has called consistently for localized textbooks and better teacher training. Research from outside Pakistan, meanwhile, has shown that materials only take on meaning through the choices teachers make while using them, and that these choices are shaped by institutional and classroom conditions rather than by teacher ability alone. What has not yet been done is to bring these two threads together and look directly at Pakistani teachers of Chinese: what they actually do with the materials they are given, why they make the choices they make, and how the conditions around them open up or close off those choices. That is the gap this study takes up, and it is also the reason the study starts from the classroom as it already is, rather than from a textbook that does not yet exist.

## Research Objectives

The general objective of this study is to investigate the recontextualization of Chinese-language teaching materials by Pakistani teachers of Chinese, and the ecological conditions in which their agency is achieved.

The specific objectives are:

1. To identify how these teachers recontextualize published Chinese-language materials in the classroom.

2. To examine the reasons they give for their decisions about those materials.

3. To analyse teacher agency in this work across the iterational, projective, and practical-evaluative dimensions.

4. To explain how classroom, institutional, examination, professional, and sociocultural conditions enable or constrain this work.
