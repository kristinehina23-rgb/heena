## 1.1  Research Background

This study begins from a documented situation, not from a general wish to “localize” Chinese teaching. Chinese-language education in Pakistan has a longer institutional history than the recent policy boom suggests. Azeem et al. (2022) note that Chinese has been taught in the country for more than forty years, but that it became widespread only in the past decade. Hanif (2023) locates the contemporary infrastructure in China–Pakistan friendship and in the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), which the paper treats as illuminating a job market for Chinese–Urdu translators and as urging Pakistani youth toward the language. The Confucius Institute at the National University of Modern Languages (NUML) is identified as the pioneer, established in 2005, followed by institutes at the University of the Punjab, the University of Agriculture Faisalabad, the University of Karachi, and the University of Sargodha—five Confucius Institutes in Hanif’s count—together with short courses in vocational training institutes. Hanif further reports 94 centres operating across Pakistan to extend Chinese learning beyond the main campuses, and a statistical analysis of HSK, HSKK, and YCT candidature that rose until 2018 and then declined. Those figures are Hanif’s programme evidence. They are not a national census of university Chinese majors, and they are not classroom observations. They are enough to fix the origin of this research: more institutes, more examination-oriented courses, and more teachers working with the same internationally compiled materials.

Universities have taken that demand into degree structures, not only into Confucius Institute short courses. Khan et al. (2022) analyse a mandatory Chinese-language policy at a major public engineering university, introduced to prepare students for Pak–China projects, Chinese university scholarships, and mobility programmes. Their interpretive policy analysis shows that most stated aims in the policy artefacts matched how policymakers and students understood those aims, but that students’ accounts of implementation diverged on the medium of instruction and on class size, with consequences for motivation and for levels of learning. Ali and David (2022), surveying 33 teachers and students in Sindh aged 20 to 40, place the same expansion inside a contested language ecology in which English is associated with power, Urdu with national cohesion, and Sindhi with regional identity. Their respondents report resistance to making Chinese compulsory from the English-speaking class and from Urdu, Seraiki, and Sindhi speech communities. Aftab and Abbasi (2024) connect university Chinese courses to CPEC as signed in 2013 and, using a BALLI questionnaire with 200 undergraduates who had completed CFL-I and CFL-II at a public-sector university, report that learners judged Chinese more difficult than other foreign languages, that few expected spoken success, that listening was easier than speaking, and that reading and writing were equally difficult. Iftikhar et al. (2024), whose PDF was not retrieved here, report from Punjab and Sindh that students generally favour Chinese learning while Sindh respondents also voice concern for local language and culture; that paper is used only as landscape. C. Wang (2022) maps Pakistani Chinese teaching as an international-communication and programme system; the dissertation PDF was not available, and the citation is landscape only. None of these studies observes how a teacher handles a prescribed page. Together they establish, with evidence, the setting in which that page is opened: mandatory as well as optional provision, examination and scholarship incentives, large classes, mixed media of instruction, and a multilingual public that does not receive Chinese as a neutral addition.

The teacher force that meets this setting is itself documented mainly as a workforce problem. Hanif (2023) describes expansion together with a shortage of local experts, dependence on volunteer teachers, uneven training, and the absence of materials written for Pakistani classrooms. Azeem et al. (2022), using a questionnaire survey of stakeholders, report significant challenges in pedagogy, curriculum and teaching material, methodology, and teachers’ professional development, and they already note that teachers are left to add or delete content from English-annotated textbooks under weak curriculum uniformity. Naheed (2026) surveys 55 local Chinese-language teachers on professional development and reports that 78.18% used *HSK Standard Course* as their main instructional resource, alongside a call for Urdu-enriched textbooks. That percentage is cited as the paper’s claim, not as re-computed data; some other percentages in the same PDF are internally inconsistent with *n* = 55. What the figure can support is modest and important: among surveyed local teachers, an HSK-oriented series already occupies the job of curriculum. What it cannot support is a claim about what those teachers do with the book in class.

That materials fact is the second part of the background, and it has a second body of evidence. Lu et al. (2024) take *HSK Standard Course* as their object in Pakistan. Drawing on a literature review, student questionnaires, and interviews with two Confucius Institute teachers (Teacher A and Teacher B), they identify cultural, linguistic, and pedagogical gaps in the series for Pakistani learners and propose Urdu annotations, cultural comparison, and updated vocabulary and teaching modules. Lu and Hanif (2025) is a follow-on article from the same programme—same first author, same corresponding author, same textbook object; it cites Lu et al. (2024) and must not be counted as independent evidence. Its method is document analysis plus further teacher interviews (Saba Shafiq, 5 August 2024, Shanghai; Abdul Basit, 30 August 2024, telephone). It reports challenges in the classroom environment, teaching materials, teacher preparedness, and student learning, and it argues that Urdu plays a key role in helping students understand Chinese. The recommendations again point to curriculum adaptation, teacher training, and the strategic use of Urdu. Read as evidence, the Lu programme shows perceived mismatch and a product remedy. It does not show, because it does not observe, how those teachers recontextualize the page minute by minute. Hanif (2023) belongs to the same authorial background for Confucius Institute landscape; it is not a third independent confirmation of the Urdu-module recommendation.

The third part of the background is therefore a discourse that the same evidence already displays. When Pakistani writing names the difficulty, it names localization, and it usually locates the remedy in a future product: Urdu glosses, cultural modules, a localized series, or more training (Lu et al., 2024; Lu & Hanif, 2025; Naheed, 2026; Azeem et al., 2022). Teachers appear as informants who can report mismatch, or as a workforce to be developed. They disappear as people who already work on the prescribed page—translating, skipping, inserting a local example, teaching to HSK, or following the book tightly because class size and the examination will not wait. Khan et al. (2022) have already shown that implementation, not only policy text, is where aims break. Azeem et al. (2022) have already shown that adding and deleting is part of teachers’ reported load. The production story still treats the classroom as a delivery site. Localization is imagined to arrive later, as a better textbook. Everyday teaching is not treated as the place where localization is already being produced, restricted, or redirected.

A neighbouring body of evidence, not written about Pakistan, makes that disappearance difficult to defend on empirical grounds. Guerrettaz and Johnston (2013), in a language-classroom ecology, found that the textbook became the de facto curriculum and structured most interaction: who spoke, about what, and with what right to leave the page. Guerrettaz et al. (2022) recast that finding as a research agenda: materials must be studied as they are used, not only as content. Rathert and Cabaroğlu (2022) treat textbook adaptation—omission, addition, substitution, modification—as a principled professional response to a felt mismatch between book and learners, not as misuse. Their 2021 case study shows the other side of the same evidence: teachers can remain strongly coursebook-led when institutional limits, learner access, planning time, and textbook structure restrict what they can change. Toledo-Sandoval (2020) found that teachers still added local references even when a book already claimed to represent local culture, because the local on the page and the local of the class are not the same construction. Yan and Weng (2026), studying Chinese high-school English teachers under new textbooks, name a pattern that is immediately readable against an HSK-led course: “we can only do addition.” Restricted work is still work on the book. Whether such work is possible is not a personal trait. Priestley et al. (2012, 2016) show agency as achieved in conditions of possibility and constraint, including creative mediation and strategic compliance; Biesta and Tedder (2007) relocate agency from a property of the person into that ecology. Bao et al. (2020), among teachers of Chinese as an additional language in Belarus, join agency to identity, community, and materials use. Dassanayake (2023) and Yasasri (2024), analysing global Chinese textbooks in Sri Lanka, document product-level neglect of first language, local culture, and reciprocal intercultural representation. These studies are analogues and warrants, not Pakistani findings. What they establish for this chapter is evidential, not theoretical decoration: a prescribed book organises the lesson; teachers already mediate it, often only by addition; that mediation is ecological; content analysis of a global Chinese series cannot stand in for classroom use.

The motivation of the research follows from the gap inside this evidence, not from an unargued preference for qualitative methods. The Pakistan studies document expansion (Hanif, 2023; Azeem et al., 2022), policy–classroom divergence on class size and medium of instruction (Khan et al., 2022), a contested language ecology (Ali & David, 2022; Aftab & Abbasi, 2024), heavy reported use of *HSK Standard Course* (Naheed, 2026), and perceived cultural, linguistic, and pedagogical misfit in that series together with a call for Urdu-supported localized materials (Lu et al., 2024; Lu & Hanif, 2025). The materials-use and ecological-agency studies document that misfit is a starting condition of practice, that the book can become the curriculum, and that what teachers can do is achieved under constraint. What no study in this set has shown, with observation plus comparison of the materials actually used, is how Pakistani teachers of Chinese recontextualize those materials in class, or how their agency is achieved, restricted, or redirected in the nested conditions of their work. If localization is already the name given to the difficulty, the first empirical task is therefore not to design another book. It is to see what teachers do with the books they have.

That task is the one stated in the abstract of this study. The study examines how Pakistani teachers of Chinese recontextualize Chinese-language teaching materials in classroom practices. Rather than treating localization solely as a matter of textbook production, it investigates the institutional, pedagogical, and professional conditions that shape teachers’ decisions about what to adapt, how to adapt it, and why. The aim is an empirically grounded account of teacher-mediated localization. In doing so, it helps researchers and educators understand the classroom as a site where localization is actively produced rather than merely received. The practical stake of that aim is already visible in the evidence above. A mandatory-language policy that does not see class size and medium of instruction will misread implementation as student unwillingness (Khan et al., 2022). A training programme that tells teachers to “adapt more” without seeing HSK, time, and collegial support mistakes a condition for a character trait (Priestley et al., 2012; Yan & Weng, 2026). A materials project that adds Urdu glosses without asking how teachers already mediate Chinese, Urdu, and English may solve a product problem and miss the pedagogic one (Lu et al., 2024; Lu & Hanif, 2025). The origin of this thesis is the evidenced expansion of Chinese teaching in Pakistan. Its background is HSK-oriented materials in use and a localization discourse that points to future books. Its motivation is to return teachers, and the ecological conditions of their work, to the centre of that discourse—and to do so with classroom evidence that the present literature does not yet supply.
