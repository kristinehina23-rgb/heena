## 1.1  Research Background

The present study originates at the point where a Chinese-language textbook enters a Pakistani university classroom. Before reaching that classroom, the material has already been shaped by decisions about legitimate language knowledge, appropriate cultural content, learner progression, classroom activities, and expected forms of instruction. Those decisions cannot anticipate every condition encountered by Pakistani teachers and learners. Once the material is used, its content has to be interpreted in relation to students’ proficiency, multilingual resources, educational histories, learning purposes, and institutional requirements. SSCI classroom research has already shown that a textbook does not move unchanged from publication to instruction. In Guerrettaz and Johnston’s (2013) study, the coursebook became the de facto curriculum and structured most classroom interaction: who spoke, about what, and with what right to leave the page. Pedagogical meaning is reconstructed through use.

This issue is located, in the present study, in Pakistani higher education, where Chinese is taught as an elective, compulsory, or professionally oriented foreign language under conditions of China–Pakistan economic and educational cooperation, including employment, scholarship, and mobility expectations connected with the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor. That is the site of the research, not a finding already established in SSCI CFL classroom studies. What SSCI research does establish is why such a site matters pedagogically. Prescribed materials often organize the content, sequence, and visible objectives of language courses (Guerrettaz & Johnston, 2013; Harwood, 2021). Policy and curriculum texts, likewise, do not translate automatically into classroom provision: teachers mediate what is given to be taught, and their space for manoeuvre varies with environmental conditions of possibility and constraint (Priestley et al., 2012). The expansion of Chinese-language provision therefore generates a pedagogical question alongside any policy question: how is Chinese actually made teachable within the conditions of Pakistani universities?

Teaching materials occupy a central position in answering that question. Materials research in SSCI journals has moved away from treating a textbook as a self-contained representation of teaching. Guerrettaz et al. (2022) define materials use as the ways in which teachers and learners employ and interact with pedagogical resources in actual learning environments, and they set a research agenda in which materials must be studied as content, as consumption, and as production (see also Harwood, 2021). An analysis of published content can show what a material makes available. It cannot show what teachers select, omit, explain, translate, reorder, supplement, or transform during instruction. Matsumoto (2019) shows, in classroom interaction, that materials are handled moment by moment, not only planned in advance. Without that window, localization remains a claim about books rather than a claim about teaching.

Classroom-based SSCI evidence confirms that the movement from written material to enacted teaching involves continuous interpretation. Li and Xu (2020) identified interconnected processes of materials use among university language teachers: identifying pedagogically relevant content, evaluating its appropriateness, adapting planned instruction, improvising in response to classroom events, reflecting on its effectiveness, and reconceiving its purposes. Teachers using the same prescribed material did not produce the same lesson, because they interpreted its affordances against different pedagogical priorities and perceptions of learners. Their choices were not unrestricted: textbook authority, institutional prescription, available resources, and learners’ expectations limited what appeared possible (Li & Xu, 2020). Z. Li (2020), in a case study of two university English teachers in China, likewise shows enactment as a process rather than a reading of the page. Materials use is therefore neither faithful transmission nor unlimited teacher creativity. It is a situated process in which published resources, professional judgement, classroom interaction, and institutional conditions meet.

This situated transformation is conceptualized in the present study as materials recontextualization. Recontextualization refers to the relocation and reinterpretation of pedagogical material as it moves from the context in which it was designed into the context in which it is taught. The process may be visible when a teacher translates an explanation, replaces a cultural example, adds locally familiar content, reorganizes an activity, changes its level of difficulty, or draws on students’ prior languages. It may also occur through less visible decisions about emphasis, sequencing, classroom language, participation, and the meanings attached to the original content. Recontextualization is therefore broader than the technical alteration of textbook exercises. It captures how teachers give materials new pedagogical functions within particular classroom circumstances.

SSCI research on internationally circulating language textbooks shows why this concept is needed. Materials designed for broad markets may embody assumptions about culture, communicative practices, learner identities, and classroom resources that do not correspond to every setting. Gray (2000) treats the ELT coursebook as a cultural artefact that teachers both censor and adapt. Toledo-Sandoval (2020) found that teachers still added local references even when a book already claimed to represent local culture, because the local on the page and the local of the class are not the same construction. Rathert and Cabaroğlu (2021), observing teachers who remained strongly coursebook-led, show the other side of the same evidence: institutional limits, learner access, planning, and textbook structure can keep practice close to the page. Yan and Weng (2026) name a further pattern under new prescribed textbooks: “we can only do addition.” Mismatch does not determine a uniform response. One teacher may replace an example; another may retain it and explain; a third may follow it closely because examinations, schedules, or institutional expectations leave limited room for change. The analytic concern is not simply whether teachers adapt, but how particular forms of recontextualization become possible and why teachers pursue some forms of action rather than others.

Localization provides the contextual vocabulary for interpreting some, but not all, of these actions. In international Chinese-language education, localization is often associated with adjusting content, explanations, examples, activities, or resources to a particular educational setting. SSCI CFL research already shows that teachers mediate language, culture, and classroom language choice rather than delivering a book intact (Q. Zhang et al., 2020; D. Wang, 2022). Localization cannot, however, be read off the place of publication. A locally produced textbook may still fail a particular class; a material produced in China may become locally meaningful through classroom mediation. In the present study, localization is a possible process and outcome of recontextualization when materials are oriented toward Pakistani learners, languages, cultures, institutions, or educational purposes. It is not assumed to be the intention behind every teacher decision, and modification is not treated automatically as evidence of pedagogical improvement.

Local teachers of Chinese occupy a particularly significant position in this process. In this study, “local teachers” refers to Pakistani teachers who teach Chinese in Pakistani universities. They work between pedagogical resources associated with international Chinese-language education and classrooms shaped by Pakistani linguistic, cultural, and institutional conditions. Knowledge of Urdu, English, regional languages, local educational expectations, and students’ experiences may offer resources for recontextualizing materials. Being local does not guarantee either the ability or the willingness to localize instruction. Professional histories, beliefs, anticipated outcomes, assessment demands, institutional hierarchies, workload, and access to resources may enable certain actions while restricting others.

This tension directs the study toward teacher agency, as that concept is used in SSCI ecological research. Agency is not a fixed characteristic that individual teachers possess. It is achieved through the interaction of teachers’ past experiences, their orientations toward possible futures, their professional judgements in the present, and the cultural, structural, and material conditions in which they work (Priestley et al., 2012; Biesta, Priestley, & Robinson, 2015; Leijen et al., 2020). Priestley et al. (2012) show that space for manoeuvre varies; Biesta, Priestley, and Robinson (2015) treat beliefs as cultural resources that do not become action without conditions in which alternatives can be spoken; L. Wang (2022) distinguishes constrained, transformative, and progressive forms of agency among English teachers under curriculum reform. Tao and Weng (2025) describe teachers who support reform ideologically and remain shackled in practice. A decision to translate a passage, introduce a Pakistani example, retain unfamiliar content, or follow a prescribed activity closely has to be understood in that environment. The same teacher may achieve different degrees or forms of agency across institutions, courses, learner groups, and moments of instruction. Agency explains neither freedom nor resistance alone; it is a way of examining how courses of action are achieved under particular conditions.

The motivation of the research follows from this SSCI evidence and from what it does not yet cover. Materials-use research in *The Modern Language Journal*, *Language Teaching*, *Language Teaching Research*, *System*, and related SSCI journals demonstrates that teaching materials are transformed through classroom enactment, often under constraint, and sometimes only by addition. Ecological-agency research in *Curriculum Inquiry*, *Teachers and Teaching*, *British Journal of Educational Studies*, *Frontiers in Psychology*, and *TESOL Quarterly* demonstrates that such transformation is an achievement in conditions, not a personal trait. CFL studies in SSCI journals show that teachers of Chinese mediate materials, language choice, and culture in other settings. What this body of evidence has not produced is an observational account, with comparison of the materials actually used, of how Pakistani teachers of Chinese recontextualize Chinese-language teaching materials, or of how ecological conditions shape the agency achieved in that process. The present study investigates that problem. Its purpose is not to evaluate teachers according to a predetermined model of localization, but to explain how localization may be produced, restricted, or redirected through their everyday work with materials.
