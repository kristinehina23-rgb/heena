## 1.1  Research Background

The present study originates at the point where a Chinese-language textbook enters a Pakistani university classroom. Before reaching that classroom, the material has already been shaped by decisions about legitimate language knowledge, appropriate cultural content, learner progression, classroom activities, and expected forms of instruction. Yet those decisions cannot anticipate every condition encountered by Pakistani teachers and learners. Once the material is used, its content must be interpreted in relation to students’ proficiency, multilingual resources, educational histories, learning purposes, and institutional requirements. The textbook consequently does not move unchanged from publication to instruction. Its pedagogical meaning is reconstructed through use.

This issue has become increasingly relevant as Chinese-language education has developed within Pakistani higher education. Economic and educational cooperation between Pakistan and China has contributed to demand for Chinese courses, particularly through employment expectations connected with the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor, scholarship opportunities, academic exchanges, and anticipated professional mobility. Pakistani universities have responded by introducing Chinese as an elective, compulsory, or professionally oriented foreign language. Evidence from a Pakistani higher education institution nevertheless shows that policy ambitions do not translate automatically into effective classroom provision. Medium of instruction, class size, course requirements, learner motivation, and institutional implementation all affect how the curriculum is experienced (Khan et al., 2022). The expansion of Chinese-language provision has therefore generated a pedagogical question alongside the policy question: how is Chinese actually made teachable within the conditions of Pakistani universities?

Teaching materials occupy a central position in answering this question because they often organize the content, sequence, and visible objectives of language courses. In some classrooms, the prescribed textbook operates as a de facto curriculum, determining what is taught and establishing expectations about how teaching should proceed (Guerrettaz & Johnston, 2013). However, materials research has progressively moved away from treating a textbook as a self-contained representation of teaching. Materials use is now understood as the ways in which teachers and learners employ and interact with pedagogical resources in actual learning environments (Guerrettaz et al., 2022). This shift matters because an analysis of published content can show what a material makes available, but it cannot show what teachers select, omit, explain, translate, reorder, supplement, or transform during instruction.

Classroom-based evidence confirms that the movement from written material to enacted teaching involves continuous interpretation. In their qualitative study of university language teachers, Li and Xu (2020) identified several interconnected processes of materials use, including identifying pedagogically relevant content, evaluating its appropriateness, adapting planned instruction, improvising in response to classroom events, reflecting on its effectiveness, and reconceiving its purposes. Teachers using the same prescribed material did not necessarily produce the same lesson because they interpreted its affordances in relation to different pedagogical priorities and perceptions of learners. At the same time, their choices were not unrestricted: textbook authority, institutional prescription, available resources, and learners’ expectations limited what appeared possible (Li & Xu, 2020). Materials use is therefore neither faithful transmission nor unlimited teacher creativity. It is a situated process in which published resources, professional judgement, classroom interaction, and institutional conditions meet.

This situated transformation is conceptualized in the present study as materials recontextualization. Recontextualization refers to the relocation and reinterpretation of pedagogical material as it moves from the context in which it was designed into the context in which it is taught. The process may be visible when a teacher translates an explanation, replaces a cultural example, adds locally familiar content, reorganizes an activity, changes its level of difficulty, or draws on students’ prior languages. It may also occur through less visible decisions about emphasis, sequencing, classroom language, participation, and the meanings attached to the original content. Recontextualization is therefore broader than the technical alteration of textbook exercises. It captures how teachers give materials new pedagogical functions within particular classroom circumstances.

Research on internationally circulating language textbooks demonstrates why this concept is needed. Materials designed for broad markets may embody assumptions about culture, communicative practices, learner identities, and classroom resources that do not correspond to every setting. Teachers consequently bridge the distance between published content and local experience by reframing topics, adding contextual knowledge, and relating unfamiliar representations to learners’ social worlds (Toledo-Sandoval, 2020). Nevertheless, the presence of a cultural or pedagogical mismatch does not determine a uniform response. One teacher may replace a textbook example; another may retain it but provide additional explanation; a third may follow it closely because examinations, course schedules, or institutional expectations leave limited room for change. The analytic concern is thus not simply whether teachers adapt materials, but how particular forms of recontextualization become possible and why teachers pursue some forms of action rather than others.

Localization provides the contextual vocabulary for interpreting some, but not all, of these actions. In international Chinese-language education, localization is often associated with adjusting teaching content, explanations, examples, activities, or resources to the needs of a particular educational setting. However, localization cannot be determined solely by where a textbook was published. A locally produced textbook may still fail to correspond to the needs of a particular group, whereas a material produced in China may become locally meaningful through teachers’ classroom mediation. In the present study, localization is therefore understood as a possible process and outcome of recontextualization when materials are oriented toward Pakistani learners, languages, cultures, institutions, or educational purposes. It is not assumed to be the intention behind every teacher decision, and modification is not treated automatically as evidence of pedagogical improvement.

Evidence from Pakistan suggests that this distinction is necessary. Studies have reported mismatches involving language of explanation, cultural familiarity, progression of difficulty, instructional design, and the availability of supporting resources. Research involving Pakistani learners and teachers, for example, found demands for Urdu-mediated explanations and more contextually accessible cultural and pedagogical support (Lu et al., 2024). Other Pakistan-focused studies have drawn attention to English-dominant educational environments, multilingual classroom realities, uneven teacher preparation, and institutional limitations (Ali & David, 2022; Azeem et al., 2022; Hanif, 2023). These studies establish that localization is a genuine concern within Pakistani Chinese-language education. Their findings, however, primarily identify needs, difficulties, perceptions, and recommendations. Such evidence cannot by itself explain how teachers respond to these conditions while planning and conducting lessons.

Local teachers of Chinese occupy a particularly significant position in this process. In this study, “local teachers” refers to Pakistani teachers who teach Chinese in Pakistani universities. They work between pedagogical resources associated with international Chinese-language education and classrooms shaped by Pakistani linguistic, cultural, and institutional conditions. Their knowledge of Urdu, English, regional languages, local educational expectations, and students’ experiences may offer resources for recontextualizing materials. Yet being local does not guarantee either the ability or the willingness to localize instruction. Professional histories, Chinese-language learning experiences, beliefs about authoritative knowledge, anticipated student outcomes, assessment demands, institutional hierarchies, workload, and access to resources may enable certain actions while restricting others.

This tension directs the study toward teacher agency. An ecological perspective does not define agency as a fixed characteristic that individual teachers possess. Agency is achieved through the interaction of teachers’ past experiences, their orientations toward possible futures, their professional judgements in the present, and the cultural, structural, and material conditions in which they work (Biesta & Tedder, 2007; Biesta, Priestley, & Robinson, 2015; Priestley et al., 2012). A teacher’s decision to translate a passage, introduce a Pakistani example, retain unfamiliar content, or follow a prescribed activity closely must therefore be understood in relation to the environment in which that decision is made. The same teacher may achieve different degrees or forms of agency across institutions, courses, learner groups, and moments of instruction. Agency consequently explains neither teacher freedom nor resistance alone; it provides a way of examining how courses of action are achieved under particular conditions.

The practical and conceptual motivation for this research emerges from the connection between these bodies of evidence. Materials-use research demonstrates that teaching materials are transformed through classroom enactment. Localization research identifies the need to relate resources to particular linguistic, cultural, and educational settings. Ecological agency theory explains how teachers’ actions are enabled and constrained through their relationships with experience, purpose, institutions, resources, and other participants. Pakistan-focused research, meanwhile, establishes the presence of material, linguistic, institutional, and professional challenges but provides limited observation of how local teachers negotiate them across lesson planning and classroom practice. The present study therefore investigates how and why local teachers of Chinese in Pakistani universities recontextualize Chinese-language teaching materials and how ecological conditions shape the agency achieved in that process. Its purpose is not to evaluate teachers according to a predetermined model of localization, but to explain how localization may be produced, restricted, or redirected through their everyday work with materials.
