## Abstract

Emergency remote teaching is often described as a platform problem. For Pakistani students holding Chinese-university places during COVID-19, it was a **home problem**. Drawing on a mixed-methods study of 97 learners who continued Chinese-language courses from Pakistan (69 men, 28 women; Likert survey plus interviews), this paper reconstructs the master’s evidence through two lenses that the original exploratory report did not use: van Dijk’s sequential model of **digital inequality** and Norton’s model of **language investment**. Collapsed item frequencies show that the domestic setting, not a preference for “online learning,” organised the experience: 81.4% reported that the home environment affected study; 78.4% reported significant psychological stress; 71.1% reported economic strain; 61.9% were unfamiliar with classroom platforms; and 73.2% did not feel proficient after the course. Three quarters rejected the claim that online study could replace in-person classes. Interview themes cluster into infrastructural exclusion (load-shedding, connectivity, devices), a crowded domestic ecology (space, family, time zone), broken pedagogical interaction, affective withdrawal, and uneven skill development in which writing was partly retained while speaking and listening were not. We argue that International Chinese Language Education along the Pakistan–China scholarship corridor cannot treat digital delivery as culturally or materially neutral. Localising Chinese teaching after the emergency means designing for **material–context fit**: power, bandwidth, household space, and multilingual mediation, not only for textbook content. Implications are drawn for CSC/CLEC programme design and for a research agenda that links digital inequality to investment in Chinese as an additional language.

**Keywords:** emergency remote teaching; Chinese as an additional language; digital inequality; language investment; Pakistan; international student mobility; home ecology; International Chinese Language Education

## 1. Introduction

When Chinese universities moved instruction online in 2020, a large group of Pakistani students did not move with the classroom. They were already, or they became, physically in Pakistan: scholarship holders and degree students whose Chinese-language courses continued from family homes, hostels, and towns marked by load-shedding, prepaid mobile data, and little quiet space (Rathore & Cao, 2024). Higher Education Commission figures circulating in that period put several thousand Pakistani students in Chinese universities on government and bilateral awards. The pedagogical object of this paper is not “online Chinese” as a planned mode of distance education. It is **emergency remote teaching** (Hodges et al., 2020) of Chinese as an additional language, delivered across a national and infrastructural border, into households that were never designed as language classrooms.

Two literatures have grown quickly and largely past one another. Research on COVID-19 and language education has documented teacher coping, task redesign, and learner anxiety, mostly in English-language classrooms or in China-based programmes (MacIntyre et al., 2020; Gacs et al., 2020; Moorhouse & Kohnke, 2021; Xia et al., 2022). Research on International Chinese Language Education (ICLE) has asked how textbooks, HSK washback, and cultural content travel into overseas programmes, including Pakistani universities and Confucius Institutes (Zhao & Huang, 2010; Gong et al., 2020). What remains thin is a study of **Pakistani students who belong to Chinese universities but study Chinese from Pakistani homes** — a South–South mobility interrupted in the opposite direction from the usual “international student in China” story.

A short mixed-methods note on this population has already been published (Rathore & Cao, 2024). That note established that the problem was real: 97 students, a 14-item questionnaire, interviews, and a list of challenges headed by electricity, internet, devices, and stress. It did not, however, offer a theory of *why* those challenges stacked, it reported some percentages as raw counts, and it stopped at a recommendation to “conduct offline classes.” The present paper keeps the same evidence and asks a different question:

> How did digital inequality in Pakistani homes structure learners’ **investment** in Chinese under emergency remote teaching, and what does that imply for localising ICLE after the emergency?

The contribution is threefold. First, empirically, we re-describe the 2024 frequencies with correct percentages and a joint quantitative–qualitative display. Second, theoretically, we join sequential digital inequality (van Dijk, 2020) to Norton’s (2013; Darvin & Norton, 2015) model of investment, treating the home as the unit of analysis rather than the platform. Third, practically, we argue that “localisation” in ICLE cannot stop at cultural representation in teaching materials. For this corridor it must include **material–context fit**: whether a lesson can be heard, asked about, and practised in the place where the student actually is.

The paper does not claim learning-gain experiments, nor that COVID-19 is still the main design problem for ICLE in 2026. The claim is narrower and, we think, more durable: the emergency made visible an inequality that programme design still needs to see.

## 2. Literature review

### 2.1 Emergency remote teaching is not online language education

Hodges et al. (2020) drew a line that language education has had reason to keep: **emergency remote teaching** (ERT) is a temporary shift under crisis, without the instructional design cycle of planned online or blended courses. Gacs, Goertler and Spasova (2020) applied that line to languages, warning against evaluating crisis teaching by the standards of mature online programmes. Reviews of language-teacher responses (Moorhouse & Kohnke, 2021) and of learner affect (MacIntyre et al., 2020) show a consistent pattern: interaction thins, assessment becomes anxious, and the domestic setting enters the lesson uninvited.

For Chinese, the same years produced a wave of papers on “suspending classes without stopping learning,” platform trials, and post-pandemic blended models (Zhang et al., 2020; Wu, 2022; Xia et al., 2022). Much of that work assumes a Chinese campus infrastructure at one end of the link and a relatively stable connection at the other. That assumption does not travel automatically to Pakistan.

### 2.2 Digital inequality, not “access”

Early “digital divide” research asked who had a computer. Later models ask a sequence (van Dijk, 2020; Helsper, 2021): motivational access, material access, skills access, usage access, and outcomes. A student may own a smartphone (material access) and still lack a quiet hour, a prepaid balance, a desktop keyboard for character input, or the literacy to move between DingTalk, a live class, and a PDF textbook (skills and usage). Warschauer (2003) argued two decades ago that language learning on screens is stratified by exactly these layers. ERT compressed them into a single domestic scene.

In Pakistan, national reports and education research during COVID-19 repeatedly named load-shedding, rural connectivity, and device sharing as structural — not accidental — features of home study (Ali, 2020; see also programme-level accounts of Confucius Institute closures and online resumption in Pakistan, e.g. discussions in Khan and colleagues’ CI scenario work). For Chinese specifically, learners inside Pakistan already faced uneven HSK provision and urban–rural gaps before 2020. Scholarship students enrolled *in China* but studying *from Pakistan* sat in a third space: they were counted as Chinese-university students, assessed by Chinese-university rules, and physically located in Pakistani digital inequality.

### 2.3 Investment, not only motivation

Motivation research can describe who “wants” to learn Chinese; it is weaker on who can **afford to be a learner** that afternoon. Norton’s (2013) construct of **investment** links identity, capital, and ideology: learners invest when they expect a return in social and material terms, and they withdraw when the classroom (or its substitute) costs more than it returns. Darvin and Norton (2015) placed investment in a digital era of shifting structures. Under ERT, the relevant structure was the household: electricity, data, siblings, and the social meaning of sitting on a phone during a live class from Wuhan or Beijing.

Piller, Zhang and Li (2020) showed that COVID-19 was also a **linguistic** crisis: who could ask for help, in which language, on which channel. Pakistani learners of Chinese typically mediate through Urdu, English, and regional languages. When a live Chinese class moved to a platform the student barely knew, the right to ask “what does this mean?” was not only a pedagogical issue. It was a question of whose language counted in the emergency.

### 2.4 ICLE, HSK, and the Pakistan corridor

China’s promotion of Chinese as an international language has long been tied to institutes, textbooks, and tests (Zhao & Huang, 2010). Pakistani interest in Chinese is bound up with CPEC, scholarships, and employment imaginaries, a pattern already visible in attitude studies during the pandemic (e.g. Pakistani survey work on reasons for learning Chinese in 2020–21). What those studies rarely capture is the **course that was supposed to produce the proficiency** — the Chinese-university language class — when the student could not be on campus.

ICLE research after COVID-19 has turned toward blended models, interaction design, and cultural adaptation (Gong et al., 2020; Zhao, 2021). Pakistani university-based work has begun to document HSK *Standard Course* use and teacher diagnosis (Lu et al., 2024; Lu & Hanif, 2025). Those studies concern programmes *in* Pakistan. The present dataset concerns Pakistanis *of* Chinese universities. The two populations share a language, a test culture, and often a textbook family; they do not share a campus. That distinction matters for localisation: adapting a book for NUML or a Confucius Institute is not the same as keeping a CSC language course alive in a Karachi or Lahore household at 21:00 after a power cut.

### 2.5 Gap

There is, then, a three-way gap. ERT research under-represents Chinese and South–South scholarship routes. Digital-inequality research under-represents additional-language classrooms. ICLE localisation research under-represents the home as a site of delivery. Rathore and Cao (2024) pointed at the phenomenon with descriptive tools. The field still needs an account of **how inequality entered investment**, and what programme design should do with that knowledge now that the emergency itself has passed.

## 3. Theoretical framework

The study is organised as a **material–context model of remote Chinese learning** (Figure 1).

**Layer 1 — Sequential digital inequality** (van Dijk, 2020). We treat electricity, devices, bandwidth, platform skills, and time as ordered resources. Absence at an earlier layer blocks later ones: no power, no live class; no platform literacy, no interaction even if the class is on.

**Layer 2 — Home ecology.** Following ecological views of language education (van Lier, 2004; Guerrettaz et al., 2021, on materials-in-use), the unit of analysis is not “the student + the app” but the household: shared rooms, family labour, gendering of domestic space, and the China–Pakistan time difference. ERT relocated the Chinese classroom into this ecology without redesigning either the lesson or the house.

**Layer 3 — Investment** (Norton, 2013; Darvin & Norton, 2015). Investment here is operationalised modestly: remaining in the live session, asking questions, practising speaking, and claiming the identity “I am becoming proficient in Chinese.” Withdrawal is also investment-relevant: attending only to “complete the time,” remaining silent, or concluding that online Chinese cannot replace the campus.

**Outcomes.** We distinguish (a) affective outcomes (stress, confidence), (b) interactional outcomes (teacher–student exchange), and (c) perceived skill outcomes (speaking, listening, writing, overall proficiency). The 2024 instrument did not measure HSK scores. Perceived proficiency is treated as an investment-relevant self-assessment, not as a test result.

The independent “support strategies” listed in the 2024 conceptual figure (clear objectives, qualified instructors, feedback, and so on) are not abandoned. They are relocated. In planned online education those supports are design features. In ERT they were **hoped-for** features that the home ecology could cancel. The research questions follow from that relocation.

**RQ1.** Which material and domestic conditions did Pakistani scholarship students report as structuring emergency remote Chinese classes?

**RQ2.** How were those conditions related to interaction, stress, and perceived proficiency?

**RQ3.** How did learners describe the consequences for speaking, listening, and writing, and for their willingness to treat online Chinese as a substitute for campus study?

**RQ4.** What does a digital-inequality × investment reading imply for localising ICLE along this corridor after ERT?

## 4. Methods

### 4.1 Design

The project used a convergent mixed-methods design (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018): a 14-item Likert questionnaire and open-ended interviews, analysed separately and then read together. Rathore and Cao (2024) called this a “triangular” design. We keep the data and tighten the label. The purpose of mixing was complementarity — frequencies to show *how widespread* a condition was; interviews to show *how it was lived* — not statistical cross-validation of the same variable.

The present paper is a **theory-driven reanalysis** of that project. No new participants were recruited. Quantitative reporting is restricted to collapsed frequencies that can be reconstructed from the 2024 tables. We do not reproduce the published item means and standard deviations, which are not credible as respondent-level statistics on a five-point scale (see §4.4). Qualitative themes are re-clustered from the published narrative. Verbatim extracts will be added from the original transcripts `[ADD]`.

### 4.2 Participants and sampling

Participants were Pakistani students enrolled in Chinese universities who attended Chinese-language classes from Pakistan during COVID-19 (n = 97; 69 men, 71.1%; 28 women, 28.9%). Recruitment was purposive, through social media, email, and personal contacts, after an explanation of aims and consent (Rathore & Cao, 2024). A pilot with about 20 students preceded the main survey.

This is not a probability sample of all Pakistani students in China. It is a theoretically relevant sample of the interrupted-mobility group. Gender imbalance likely reflects both the CSC/Pakistani STEM-heavy enrolment pattern and network sampling. We do not generalise percentages to the national population; we generalise the **mechanism** (home ecology → blocked investment) with caution.

**Interviews.** Three published prompts were:

1. What issues did you face during online classes?  
2. How did you feel after missing an online class and then attending the next lecture?  
3. After the online Chinese course, how do you feel about speaking and writing?

Questions were explained in national and local Pakistani languages (Rathore & Cao, 2024). That mediation is itself evidence for RQ1: the research conversation, like the classroom, had to be translated.

Those values cannot be student-level means on a 1–5 item; they appear to describe the distribution of five frequency bins. ## 5. Findings

### 5.1 The home, not the platform, was the main classroom condition (RQ1)

Table 3 in the accompanying `tables.md` lists collapsed frequencies. Three items locate the problem in **place and resource**, not in a pedagogical fashion for online study.

- **Home environment affected online learning:** 79/97 (81.4%) agreed.  
- **Economically affected during online classes:** 69/97 (71.1%) agreed.  
- **Internet access whenever classes ran:** 55/97 (56.7%) disagreed.  
- **Familiar with online classroom platforms:** 60/97 (61.9%) disagreed.

A majority (59/97, 60.8%) still judged class *duration* as suitable, and 60/97 (61.9%) were satisfied with the overall duration of their Chinese learning. Time on the timetable was not the scarce resource. **Time that could be used as study time** — powered, connected, quiet — was. 56/97 (57.7%) agreed that the time zone affected classes: live Chinese-university slots landed on Pakistani domestic schedules.

Interview material names the same stack: electricity and load-shedding; internet; “latest technology” expected but unavailable at home; lack of quiet study space; family issues. These are sequential in van Dijk’s sense. Load-shedding cancels material access; shared phones weaken usage; an unfamiliar platform (61.9% not familiar) cancels skills access even when the stream is on.

### 5.2 Stress was common; replacement of campus study was rejected (RQ2)

- **Online learning created significant psychological stress:** 76/97 (78.4%) agreed.  
- **Prefer online classes:** 62/97 (63.9%) disagreed.  
- **Online learning can replace in-person classes:** 75/97 (77.3%) disagreed.  
- **Satisfied that Chinese classes were fully online:** only 49/97 (50.5%) agreed — the most split of the “delivery” items.  
- **Actively interacted with the teacher online:** 56/97 (57.7%) agreed, which means a large minority did not.

Read together, students did not describe themselves as converts to online Chinese. Half could accept that classes *were* online; three quarters refused the stronger claim that online *should replace* the campus. Interaction with teachers was only a bare majority experience. In investment terms, many students remained present without remaining **participants**.

Interview themes match the affective numbers: mental disturbance; shyness when responding to the teacher; the feeling that other students were more competent; confusion when a missed live session made the next one unintelligible; “not focused, irresponsible, not interested, just focusing to complete the time period” (paraphrase of Rathore & Cao, 2024, §4.2). Completing time is the opposite of investment. It is attendance as compliance.

### 5.3 Perceived proficiency stayed low, and skills split (RQ3)

- **Feel proficient in Chinese after the course:** 71/97 (73.2%) disagreed.  
- **Online learning can help improve Chinese proficiency:** 53/97 (54.6%) disagreed.

The 2024 qualitative summary is more precise than the items. After the course, students “just learned alphabet in Chinese, some words for speaking… adoption the accent of Chinese. Not feeling proficient in speaking, listening, but writing somehow” (Rathore & Cao, 2024, p. 199). We take this as an **uneven skill profile**, not as a global failure of will.

The profile is theoretically expected. Character writing and copied homework can be done asynchronously after a power cut. Live speaking and listening cannot. ERT therefore did not merely “reduce proficiency”; it **reallocated practice** toward what a crowded, intermittently powered home can host. Assessment and cheating were also named in interviews: when the only remaining visible output is written work done at home, the incentive structure shifts.

Missing a class had a compounding effect (interview prompt 2). Chinese-university language courses are typically cumulative. In ERT, the recording — if it existed — still required data and time. The next live session then became a site of public incomprehension, feeding the shyness and peer-comparison already reported.

| 81.4% home environment; 57.7% time zone | No quiet space; family interruption; clocks aligned to China | The learner identity has to be performed *inside* competing domestic identities |

| 78.4% stress; shyness, peer comparison | Affective withdrawal | High cost, low imagined return → reduced investment |
| 71.1% economic strain | Family issues; “complete the time” | Chinese as a scholarship duty, not a practice one can fund that day |
| 73.2% not proficient; speaking/listening < writing | Alphabet, isolated words, writing “somehow” | Outcome layer: ERT produces a written residue, not oral capital |
| 77.3% reject replacement of in-person classes | Limited interactivity | Ideology: campus Chinese remains the legitimate classroom |

The 2024 “support strategies” (quality materials, qualified instructors, feedback) do not disappear from this picture. They become **counterfactual**: students can recognise what a good Chinese class would need and still be unable to inhabit it from home. That is why a motivation-only reading (“they were not enthusiastic”) is insufficient. Enthusiasm was measured (α = 0.835) but the dominant story in both strands is **constraint**.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 ERT as a stress test of South–South language mobility

CSC and related awards imagine a campus: dormitory, classroom, extra-curricular Chinese, and an HSK calendar. ERT kept the assessment imagination and removed the campus. The Pakistani home then had to impersonate a Chinese-university language classroom. It could not. The high stress figure (78.4%) is not surprising as pandemic psychology; it is specific as **language-learning psychology** once speaking and listening — the skills that most require a live other — are the ones the home cannot reliably host (cf. MacIntyre et al., 2020, on teacher affect; the learner analogue here is quieter, more ashamed, more likely to go silent).

### 6.2 Digital inequality is sequential in this dataset

The findings fit van Dijk’s order better than a single “poor internet” headline. Power and data sit underneath platform literacy; platform literacy sits underneath interaction; interaction sits underneath perceived oral proficiency. A policy that donates recordings, or that trains teachers in breakout rooms, addresses later layers first. For this corridor, the earlier layers are household electricity and the cost of remaining online for a two-hour slot.

### 6.3 Investment explains “just complete the time”

Norton’s model is often used to celebrate learners who insist on speaking. It is equally a model of **rational withdrawal**. If the imagined return of Chinese (scholarship continuity, CPEC employment, a future in China) remains high, but the daily cost (data, conflict over space, public failure in the next lesson) remains higher, students protect the award by staying logged in and protect the self by not speaking. The 57.7% who said they interacted with the teacher, set against 73.2% who denied proficiency, is consistent with that split: some interaction, little claimed competence.

Darvin and Norton (2015) argued that digital spaces reconfigure capital. Here the reconfiguration was regressive. Campus Chinese offers embodied oral capital. Home ERT offered, at best, written traces and, at worst, a record of absence.

### 6.4 Localisation is material, not only cultural

ICLE debates on localisation often turn on whether a textbook should replace Beijing hutongs with local names, or whether Spring Festival content needs Islamic-context mediation (a line of work we have pursued separately and do not merge here). This dataset pushes localisation one layer down. Even a perfectly “culturally adapted” lesson fails if it is a synchronous oral lesson delivered into load-shedding. **Material–context fit** — whether this component of the course can be used by these learners in this programme ecology — is the same logic that the first author’s doctoral work applies to *HSK Standard Course* in Pakistani universities. The COVID evidence is the extreme case of misfit: the “programme ecology” was a Chinese-university timetable; the “users” sat in Pakistani houses; the “material” was a live class that assumed campus conditions.

### 6.5 Gender, sampling, and what we cannot say

The sample is 71% male. We do not have item breakdowns by gender. Pakistani domestic space is gendered; a claim that “the home environment” was the main barrier almost certainly hides different barriers for women (privacy, device sharing, care work). That is a limitation, not a minor one. Network sampling also likely over-recruited students who remained reachable on social media — that is, students who had *some* connectivity. The true infrastructural exclusion is probably harsher than 56.7% “no reliable internet.”

## 7. Implications

### 7.1 For CSC, CLEC, and Chinese universities hosting Pakistani students

1. **Treat disruption-ready Chinese as a design problem, not a platform subscription.** Asynchronous oral alternatives (short voice tasks with delayed feedback; SMS/WhatsApp voice notes that survive low bandwidth) should be in the language-course handbook, not invented in a crisis.  
2. **Do not assess emergency cohorts as if they had campus oral hours.** If speaking was structurally un-practisable, proficiency claims and progression decisions should not rest on oral-equivalent standards without adjustment.  
3. **Budget data and devices as language-learning resources** for scholarship students who are offshore, in the same way that textbooks are budgeted.  
4. **Name time zone and load-shedding in timetabling.** A 08:00 Beijing slot is a domestic-night slot in Pakistan; adding “attendance” to it measures electricity as much as diligence.

### 7.2 For Pakistani universities and Confucius Institutes

The same households send students to CIs *and* to Chinese universities. Hybrid CI teaching that still assumes uninterrupted Zoom reproduces the same misfit. Localisation workshops for Pakistani Chinese teachers should include **infrastructure audits** (power, device, mediation language) beside textbook adaptation.

### 7.3 For research

Future work should (a) recover item-level data for this cohort and test whether infrastructure items predict stress and proficiency once gender and HSK are controlled; (b) compare CSC-home learners with CI-in-Pakistan learners in the same cities; (c) follow oral/written skill splits with actual tasks, not only self-report. The first author’s ongoing work on *HSK Standard Course* fit in Pakistani universities is one such continuation: it keeps the material–context question and moves it from emergency delivery to ordinary curriculum.

8. **Invalid means in the 2024 table are discarded, not repaired.** We would rather report less than report a mean of 19 on a five-point item.

## 9. Conclusion

Pakistani scholarship students who learned Chinese from home during COVID-19 did not mainly fail to “adapt to online learning.” Their homes were asked to replace a Chinese campus and were not equipped to do so. Digital inequality entered the lesson as power, data, devices, platform literacy, and domestic space; investment left the lesson as silence, compliance, stress, and a skill profile tilted toward writing. Three quarters of the sample refused the idea that this arrangement could replace in-person classes. That refusal should be taken seriously in ICLE programme design. Localising Chinese teaching for this corridor means fitting materials *and delivery* to the contexts in which learners actually sit. The emergency has receded. The inequality it filmed has not.
